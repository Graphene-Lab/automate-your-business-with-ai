# Chapter 12 — Where AI Can Help Your Business

## In Simple Words

AI is not a single tool that fits every job. It is a set of capabilities, and each one fits some tasks well and others badly. The first real question is not "should we use AI?" It is "where, exactly, will it help us?"

Think of AI like power tools in a workshop. A drill is wonderful for making holes. It is useless for stirring paint, and dangerous for cutting bread. The skill is not owning the tools. The skill is knowing which tool matches which job. AI is the same. It is strong at some things and weak at others, and your advantage comes from pointing it at the right tasks.

What is AI genuinely good at, in business terms? Three broad things. It can **read and understand** unstructured information — emails, invoices, contracts, notes — the kind of text and images that used to need human eyes. It can **write and produce** text, summaries, drafts, and answers at a speed no person can match. And it can **spot patterns and suggest decisions** from large amounts of data — which customer is likely to leave, which machine is likely to fail, which stock will run out.

What is AI not good at? It is not good at tasks that need firm facts it does not have, at real accountability, at physical work, or at judging situations it has never seen. It makes mistakes. It can be confidently wrong. So you point it at work where a fast draft you can check is valuable, not at work where a single error ruins you.

This chapter is the map. It shows you how to look at your whole business, find the activities where AI fits, and decide where to start. The method is simple and works whether you run a shop, a firm, or a factory. Later chapters cover the tools and the how-to. This one answers the "where."

The single most useful idea here is the **impact/ease matrix**, and this book keeps its full explanation in this chapter. Once you learn it, you can look at any task and know roughly whether to do it now, later, or never.

## A Bit of History

**1960s–1980s: automation meant factories.** For decades, "automation" meant machines doing physical work on an assembly line. Office work and thinking work were untouched. If you were not in manufacturing, automation was someone else's story.

**1990s: software entered the office.** Spreadsheets, databases, and early workflow tools automated calculations and record-keeping. This was automation by rules: you told the software exactly what to do, step by step. It was powerful but rigid. Anything that did not fit the rules still needed a person.

**2000s: business process management.** Companies began mapping their processes on paper and diagrams, looking for waste and bottlenecks. The discipline of "map the process first" came from here. It is still the right first step for AI today.

**2010s: AI reached language and images.** Machine learning got good enough to read text, recognise images, and handle messy inputs that rule-based software could not touch. Suddenly the "thinking work" that resisted automation became reachable. This is the shift that opened AI to ordinary businesses, not just factories.

**2020s: generative AI made it universal.** Tools that write, summarise, and answer in plain language arrived. Now a business with no engineers could point AI at emails, reports, and customer messages. The question moved from "can we?" to "where should we?" — which is exactly this chapter.

The lesson from this history: every wave of automation first touched the most repetitive, rule-like work, then moved toward messier tasks as the tools improved. AI follows the same path. The best places to start are still the repetitive and predictable ones — now with tools that handle far more variety than before.

## Curiosity

### 12.5 The company that automated 96% of its planning

One of the clearest proofs of what "where AI helps" looks like in practice: a healthcare services company automated **96% of its planning work** and cut **waiting times by 57%**. Planning — matching staff to shifts, routes, and schedules — is exactly the kind of repetitive, rule-heavy, time-consuming task AI handles well, and the gain was not just saved hours but faster service for the people waiting.

This book tells the full TridentCare case in [Chapter 27](ch27-customer-care-and-support.md), including how they did it and what they learned. The point here is only the headline: when you find the right activity, the result can be dramatic. ## A Real Business Example

### A five-person accounting firm finds its real target

A small accounting firm of five people spent a large part of every week on one thing: collecting client documents and entering data. Clients emailed receipts, bank statements, and invoices in every format imaginable. Two staff members spent hours each day opening attachments, reading them, and typing figures into the accounting system.

They did not start by buying anything. They started by mapping. They wrote down every activity the firm did in a week and marked, for each one, how repetitive it was and how much time it took. The document-and-data task stood out immediately: high repetition, high hours, low judgment. It was a textbook target.

They tried AI on just that piece. The tool read incoming documents, pulled out the figures, and filled the system. Staff checked the result instead of typing it. The work did not vanish, but it shrank from hours to minutes per client, and the two staff moved to advisory work the firm could bill for.

Notice what made this work. They did not try to "use AI everywhere." They found one activity that matched the pattern — repetitive, predictable, high-volume, low-risk if checked — and aimed carefully. That is the whole method of this chapter in one small firm.

## How to Do It

### 12.1 Mapping business processes

You cannot choose where AI helps until you can see what your business actually does. That means mapping your processes. A process is just a repeating set of steps that turns an input into an output: an order comes in, you pick, pack, ship, and invoice. A CV arrives, you screen, interview, and decide.

To map a process, write down the steps in order. For each step, note four things:

- **What triggers it.** What starts this step? An email, a form, a customer click.
- **What happens.** The actual action — read, type, decide, approve, send.
- **Who does it.** Which person or role.
- **How long it takes and how often.** Minutes per task, tasks per day or week.

Do this for your main processes. Keep it simple — a list or a simple table is enough. Do not buy software to map; a spreadsheet or paper works. The goal is to see, on one page, where the time goes and where the repetition lives.

Two tips. First, map what people *actually* do, not what the official procedure says. The gap between the two is often where the waste hides. Second, talk to the people doing the work. They know exactly which steps are painful, repetitive, and slow. They are your best source.

Once the map is done, you have a list of activities you can test against the filters in the next section. The mapping itself often reveals opportunities you had stopped noticing because they were invisible inside daily routine.

### 12.2 Repetitive, predictable, and decision-making activities

Not all work suits AI equally. Three qualities make an activity a strong candidate.

**Repetitive.** The same task, over and over. Repetition is opportunity because a tool that learns the task once can repeat it cheaply. A task you do once a year is rarely worth automating; one you do a hundred times a day almost always is. Repetition is the single strongest signal.

**Predictable.** The task follows a recognizable pattern, with clear inputs and a clear idea of a correct output. "Turn this invoice into these fields" is predictable. "Invent a new marketing campaign" is not. Predictability means the AI has a stable target to aim at. Some variation is fine — modern AI handles variety — but the underlying shape must be stable.

**Decision-making (with data).** Tasks where you make a judgment using information can benefit from AI's pattern-spotting. "Which of these leads should we call first?" "Which invoice looks unusual?" "Which customer might cancel?" AI can rank, flag, and suggest. The key is that a decision is being made from data, and a faster or better suggestion has value.

Now the flip side. Activities that are **one-off, creative, ambiguous, or require firm accountability** are weak candidates. AI can assist them, but it should not own them. A first draft of a strategy memo is fine for AI. The final strategic decision is yours.

A simple test for any activity on your map: *Is it repetitive? Is it predictable? Does it involve a decision from data?* If you can answer yes to at least two, it is worth a closer look. If all three are no, leave it to people.

### 12.3 The typical areas: administration, sales, marketing, customer care, HR, operations, IT, leadership

Every business has roughly the same set of functions, and each has its own good AI targets. Here is where AI typically helps, area by area. This is a survey; the deep treatment of each is in Part VII of this book.

**Administration and finance.** Document reading, invoice processing, data entry, expense checks, report drafting, reconciliation. High repetition, high volume — among the best starting points. Detailed in [Chapter 25](ch25-administration-and-finance.md).

**Sales.** Lead scoring, drafting outreach emails, summarising calls, updating the CRM, suggesting next actions. AI is strong at the writing and the ranking. See [Chapter 26](ch26-sales-and-marketing.md).

**Marketing.** Drafting copy, generating variations, summarising campaign results, segmenting audiences, personalising messages. Creative assist, not creative replacement. Also [Chapter 26](ch26-sales-and-marketing.md).

**Customer care.** Answering common questions, drafting replies, routing tickets, summarising history, building knowledge bases. One of the highest-value areas because volume is high and many questions repeat. The TridentCare case sits in [Chapter 27](ch27-customer-care-and-support.md).

**HR and people.** Screening CVs against a role, drafting job posts, answering employee policy questions, summarising feedback. Careful: this touches personal data and bias, so the duties in [Chapter 4](ch04-ethical-ai-doing-the-right-thing.md) and [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md) apply strongly. See [Chapter 28](ch28-hr-and-people.md).

**Operations and production.** Demand forecasting, scheduling, inventory, quality checks, maintenance prediction. Decision-from-data tasks with real money attached. [Chapter 29](ch29-operations-and-production.md).

**IT.** Code assistance, ticket triage, documentation, monitoring alerts. [Chapter 30](ch30-it-and-leadership.md).

**Leadership.** Summarising reports, drafting communications, surfacing trends. AI as a staff function for you, not a decision-maker. Also [Chapter 30](ch30-it-and-leadership.md).

The pattern across all areas is the same: aim at the repetitive, predictable, data-driven pieces, and keep a human on the judgment and the accountability.

### 12.4 The "impact/ease" matrix: where to start

This is the canonical home of the impact/ease matrix. Learn it once and use it everywhere.

The matrix is a simple way to rank every candidate activity so you know what to do first. You score each activity on two axes.

**Impact (vertical axis).** How much value you gain if you automate this activity well. Impact can be time saved, money saved, errors avoided, faster service, or revenue gained. Ask: *if this activity got dramatically better, how much would it help the business?* High impact means it matters a lot. Low impact means it barely moves the needle.

**Ease (horizontal axis).** How easy it is to automate this activity with AI. Ease depends on how repetitive and predictable the task is, how clean the data is, how simple it is to connect the tool, and how low the risk is if it makes a mistake. Ask: *how hard is it to do this, and how much can I safely let AI handle?* High ease means simple, low-risk, ready data. Low ease means messy, risky, or hard to connect.

Now plot every candidate activity into one of four boxes.

**High impact, high ease — Start now.** These are your quick wins. They matter a lot and they are easy to do. This is where your first projects live. Do these immediately. They pay back fast and build confidence.

**High impact, low ease — Plan carefully.** These matter but are hard. They are worth doing, but not first. Put them in a longer project, budget for the difficulty, and tackle them after you have proven the approach with quick wins. Do not start here; you will burn money and goodwill on a hard problem before you have experience.

**Low impact, high ease — Do opportunistically.** Easy but not very valuable. Fine to do when you have spare time, or as a gentle learning exercise, but do not spend real budget here. They are distractions if you treat them as priorities.

**Low impact, low ease — Ignore.** Hard and not worth it. Skip these entirely. They are traps that look interesting but never pay off.

The discipline of the matrix is what makes it powerful. Most people's instinct is to chase the impressive high-impact, low-ease projects because they sound important. The matrix tells you to resist that and start with high-impact, high-ease instead. You build momentum, learn, and fund the harder work later.

To use it: take your mapped activities, score each one low/medium/high on impact and on ease, and place it in a box. List the "start now" box first. That list is your first set of priorities. Keep the scoring honest — it is normal to want to inflate impact and hide difficulty. Resist both.

A caution on ease: ease is not only about the technology. A task can be technically easy but hard because the data is messy, the staff resist it, or the risk of error is high. Count those in. True ease includes people, data, and risk, not just "can the tool do it."

## Ethics and Responsibility

Choosing where to use AI is itself an ethical choice, not just a financial one.

**Watch the high-stakes areas.** HR screening, customer decisions, anything touching people's opportunities or money carries risk. A wrong AI decision there can harm a real person. In those areas, AI should assist, never decide alone. The fairness and transparency duties in [Chapter 4](ch04-ethical-ai-doing-the-right-thing.md) apply, and the law in [Chapter 5](ch05-rules-and-legal-responsibility.md) may classify some of these as higher-risk uses.

**Do not automate away accountability.** Even when AI does the work, a human remains responsible for the outcome. Never use "the AI did it" to escape responsibility. Someone must own every automated decision that affects a person.

**Consider the people whose work changes.** Automating a task changes the lives of the people who did it. Do it with honesty and a plan for them, not as a surprise. The change-management side is in [Chapter 21](ch21-managing-change-in-your-company.md).

**Be honest about what AI can and cannot do.** When you pick a target, do not oversell it internally or to customers. If the tool is a draft-maker, call it that. Setting honest expectations is part of responsible use.

## Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Starting with the hardest project.** The impressive, complex automation is a trap. Start with a quick win, not a moonshot.
2. **Automating a broken process.** If a process is messy or wrong, automating it just makes bad work faster. Fix the process before you automate it.
3. **Chasing AI everywhere at once.** Spreading thin across many small uses wastes effort. Pick a few high-impact, high-ease targets and do them well.
4. **Ignoring the ease side.** A high-impact task with messy data and high risk is not actually easy. Count data quality and risk in your ease score.
5. **Choosing by fashion, not fit.** "Everyone is using AI for X" is not a reason. Choose by your own map and matrix.
6. **Forgetting the human in the loop.** In any task that affects a person, keep a human checking. Full automation of high-stakes decisions is reckless.
7. **Skipping the mapping.** Picking targets by gut feel, without mapping, means you miss the best ones and pick the wrong ones.
8. **Confusing a demo with a workflow.** A tool that dazzles in a demo may not fit your real, messy process. Test against your actual activities.

## Practical Exercise

### 12.6 List 10 activities you could automate

Set aside an hour and do this simple exercise.

Write down **ten activities** in your business that you could imagine automating. They can come from your process map, or from what annoys you daily. For each one, add three quick notes:

- **How repetitive?** Roughly how many times per week do you or your team do it?
- **How predictable?** Can you describe the correct output clearly? Yes / sort of / no.
- **What would AI do?** One phrase: read it, draft it, rank it, check it.

Example entries:

1. Reading incoming invoices and entering figures — 50×/week — yes — extract and fill.
2. Replying to common customer emails — 80×/week — sort of — draft a reply.
3. Summarising weekly sales reports — 1×/week — yes — summarize.
4. Screening CVs for a role — 20×/week — sort of — rank against criteria.
5. Scheduling staff shifts — 2×/week — yes — propose a schedule.

Keep going until you have ten. Do not judge them yet; just collect. The goal is a pool of candidates.

Next, take your ten and score each one **impact** (low/medium/high) and **ease** (low/medium/high) using the matrix from section 12.4. Place each in a box. Circle everything in the **high-impact, high-ease** box. Those circles are your first priorities. You now have, from one hour of work, a short honest list of where to start.

## Checklist

### 12.7 Choosing your first priorities

Before you commit to a first AI project, check these.

- [ ] **You have mapped your main processes** and can see where time and repetition live.
- [ ] **You have at least 10 candidate activities** written down.
- [ ] **Each candidate is scored on impact and ease** using the matrix.
- [ ] **Your top picks are high-impact AND high-ease**, not just impressive.
- [ ] **The chosen activity is repetitive and predictable**, with a clear correct output.
- [ ] **The data needed is available and reasonably clean** (see [Chapter 14](ch14-data-the-raw-material.md)).
- [ ] **The risk of an error is acceptable**, or a human check is built in.
- [ ] **A human stays accountable** for any decision that affects a person.
- [ ] **You are not automating a broken process** — the process is sound first.
- [ ] **You can measure the result** — time saved, errors cut, or money gained (see [Chapter 16](ch16-goals-costs-and-return-on-investment.md)).
- [ ] **You resisted the tempting-but-hard project** and chose a genuine quick win.
- [ ] **The people affected know and are not blindsided.**

If your top pick fails several of these, move to the next one. Your first project should be the easiest high-value thing, not the most exciting.

## Key Takeaways

- AI fits repetitive, predictable, data-driven tasks; it is weak at one-off, ambiguous, or fully accountable work, so aim it at the right jobs.
- Map your processes first — you cannot choose where AI helps until you can see what your business actually does and where the repetition lives.
- The impact/ease matrix sorts every candidate into start-now, plan-carefully, do-when-idle, or ignore; begin with high-impact, high-ease quick wins.
- Resist the impressive hard project; start small, prove the method, and fund the harder work later.
- In any task that touches a person, keep a human checking and accountable — automation never removes responsibility.
