# Chapter 39 — Frequently Asked Questions

Small-business owners and professionals ask the same questions about AI, in nearly the same words, every time. This chapter collects the most common ones and answers them plainly and honestly — no sales pitch, no scare tactics. Each answer points to the chapter that covers the topic in full, so you can go deeper where it matters to you. Read the ones that match your worry. You will find that most fears have a practical answer, and most "too hard" problems have a small first step.

## Does it cost too much?

Less than you fear, if you start small. For a small business, the real cost is rarely the software. Many useful AI tools cost a modest monthly fee per person — often in the range of a few tens of euros or dollars per user — and some basic ones are free. A simple chat assistant or a drafting tool will not break your budget.

The costs people overlook are time and training. Setting up a pilot, teaching staff, and checking the output take hours. That is the real investment, and it is the part that pays back. Self-hosting — running the AI on your own computers — needs hardware and skills, so it costs more up front; see [Chapter 8](ch08-self-hosting-keep-your-data-under-control.md).

The rule is to start with a cheap, small pilot before any big spend. [Chapter 16](ch16-goals-costs-and-return-on-investment.md) shows how to work out the return so you never pay more than the thing is worth. Most small projects cost a few hundred, not a fortune.

## Do I need a technician?

For a first pilot, usually no. Modern AI tools are built for normal users, not coders. If you can use a spreadsheet or write an email, you can use a drafting assistant, a chat helper, or a document summariser. The skills that matter — asking clearly, checking the answer, judging when to trust — are explained in [Chapter 37](ch37-the-changing-nature-of-work.md), and none require programming.

You may want technical help for specific jobs: connecting AI to your existing systems securely, running it on your own machines, or handling sensitive data. Those are real tasks, and a trusted IT partner is worth having for them. But you do not need one to begin.

The honest framing: you need a curious owner and a clear plan more than you need a technician. [Chapter 38](ch38-a-90-day-action-plan.md) gives a plan a non-technical person can run. Bring technical help in for the wiring, not for the idea.

## Will I lose my data?

Your data is not likely to vanish, but you must control where it goes. The real risk is not that AI deletes your files. It is that you paste sensitive information into a tool that stores it, shares it, or uses it to train its model without your clear understanding. That is a privacy and control problem, not a storage problem.

The protection is simple: know where your data goes before you put it anywhere. Read what a tool does with what you feed it. Do not paste customer personal data, financial records, or secrets into a free public tool. Use tools that let you control retention, or run the AI yourself if control is critical. [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md) covers the privacy rules, and [Chapter 20](ch20-implementing-ai-securely.md) covers safe setup.

Keep your own backups as you always would. AI does not change the need for a backup; it only adds a new place your data might travel. Control the travel, and you keep the data.

## Will AI make me fire someone?

Not by itself, and that is the wrong goal. AI changes the tasks inside a job; it does not decide your headcount. The realistic pattern, seen across the cases in this book, is that people move from *doing* a repetitive task to *supervising* the machine that does it — as in the TridentCare scheduling story in [Chapter 27](ch27-customer-care-and-support.md).

When a task gets automated, the honest question is what the person does next. Usually the answer is better work: more clients, more judgement, more of the human tasks that pay best. The "Headcount Zero" idea of a company with no employees is a thought experiment, not a plan — [Chapter 17](ch17-choosing-tools-without-being-fooled.md) explains why.

If automation truly removes a role, treat the person with respect and plan their next step. Using AI as a quiet excuse to cut staff without support is both unfair and short-sighted. Aim for more capability per person, not fewer people. [Chapter 4](ch04-ethical-ai-doing-the-right-thing.md) covers the duty.

## Where do I start?

Start with one small, painful, repetitive task — not with a strategy document. Look at your week and find something you do over and over that annoys you: answering the same client questions, drafting the same reports, matching invoices. That is your first pilot.

Then follow a fixed plan. [Chapter 38](ch38-a-90-day-action-plan.md) lays out ninety days: observe the work, choose one small test, run it with a human check on everything, and measure the result. [Chapter 18](ch18-your-first-pilot-project.md) explains the pilot method itself. To rank which task is worth doing first, use the impact-and-ease thinking in [Chapter 12](ch12-where-ai-can-help-your-business.md).

The key is to go small and reversible. One process, a few people, a short test. A small win builds confidence and teaches you how to run the next one. A big first project is how good money gets wasted. Pick the small win today.

## How long does it take?

A first pilot takes about ninety days, and that is by design. Long enough to learn something real and see a result; short enough to keep urgency. The four phases — observe, choose, run, measure — are set out week by week in [Chapter 38](ch38-a-90-day-action-plan.md).

You can see a first useful result in a few weeks. Setting up a drafting tool and training one or two people can show a time saving inside a month. Proving it is worth spreading takes the full quarter, because you need a baseline, a real run, and an honest measurement.

Do not expect the new way to feel natural in three weeks. The "21 days to form a habit" idea is a myth; real habits take closer to two months, as [Chapter 38](ch38-a-90-day-action-plan.md) explains. Plan for the real curve. After the first pilot, each new one goes faster because you already know the method.

## Which tools should I choose?

Choose the task first, the tool last. The most common mistake is falling for a shiny product and then hunting for a place to use it. Start from the problem you defined, then pick the tool that fits it. [Chapter 17](ch17-choosing-tools-without-being-fooled.md) covers how to choose without being fooled.

A few plain rules. Prefer tools that fit what you already use over ones that demand a new system. Prefer a tool that keeps a human in the loop over one that acts alone. Prefer clear pricing over hidden costs. Try before you buy with a small pilot, not a company-wide rollout.

There is no single best tool. A drafting assistant, a chat agent, and a document summariser are different jobs. Match the tool to the task. And remember the tool is only half of it — the pairing of the tool with a trained person is what produces the result, as [Chapter 37](ch37-the-changing-nature-of-work.md) explains.

## How do I choose a vendor?

Judge a vendor on honesty and fit, not on the demo. A polished demonstration tells you almost nothing; a clean input is not a test. Ask what the tool did when it did not know the answer, and how many real cases it has handled. [Chapter 1](ch01-a-short-history-of-ai.md) explains why the demo-to-production gap is normal and how to probe it.

Ask direct questions. Where does my data go, and who can see it? Can I export my data and leave easily? What happens when the AI is wrong — is there a human check? What is the total cost, including setup and training, not just the licence? Who is responsible if it makes a mistake?

Watch for red flags: pressure to sign now, vague answers about data, claims of "fully automatic" with no human review, and prices that hide the real cost. A good vendor welcomes these questions. A bad one dodges them. Start small and reversible so a wrong choice costs you a pilot, not the company.

## Is it safe?

It can be, if you set it up with care. AI is not safe or unsafe in itself. Safety depends on how you use it. The main risks are wrong answers that reach a customer unchecked, data sent somewhere you did not intend, and over-trust because the output sounds confident. Each has a plain defence.

The core defence is a human check on anything that matters. Never let AI output reach a customer, a payment, or a legal decision without a person approving it. Keep personal data under control and know where it goes. Treat every output as a draft to verify, not a finished answer. [Chapter 20](ch20-implementing-ai-securely.md) covers secure setup, and [Chapter 6](ch06-cybersecurity-in-the-ai-era.md) covers the security threats to watch for.

Remember that fluent output is not true output. A system that writes well can still be wrong, and it will not always tell you. The safety sits with you, the human who checks. Do that, and AI is about as safe as any other tool you already use.

## What does the law say?

The law is real and it is tightening, but it is manageable if you follow a few principles. In the European Union, the AI Act sets rules by risk level: some uses are banned, some carry heavy duties, and most everyday business uses fall in a light category. [Chapter 5](ch05-rules-and-legal-responsibility.md) is the canonical home for the AI Act.

If you handle personal data of people in the EU, the GDPR applies, and it applies whether or not AI is involved. You need a lawful basis, you must tell people what you do with their data, and you must protect it. [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md) covers GDPR in plain terms.

The practical rule: know what data you handle, be transparent about how you use it, and keep a human responsible for decisions that affect people. Where AI makes a decision about a person — hiring, credit, service — extra care and often extra disclosure apply. For a small business, the safest path is to use AI for drafts and support, keep humans deciding, and follow the privacy rules you already follow for other data.

## Can I use AI without giving my data to anyone?

Yes, by running the AI on your own computers. This is called **self-hosting**: instead of sending your data to a company's servers over the internet, you run the model on hardware you control, so the data never leaves your machine. [Chapter 8](ch08-self-hosting-keep-your-data-under-control.md) is the canonical home for self-hosting and tools like Ollama that make it practical.

Self-hosting gives you the strongest control. Your data stays home. Nothing is shared, sold, or used to train someone else's model. For sensitive work — legal, medical, financial, or anything you cannot let leave the building — this is the most protective option.

The trade-off is cost and effort. Self-hosting needs capable hardware, setup, and some technical help, and the models you can run yourself may be smaller than the biggest online ones. For many small businesses, a controlled cloud tool with the right settings is enough. For data that truly cannot leave, self-hosting is the answer. Weigh what you must protect against what you can spend.

## Is self-hosting really safer?

It is safer for one specific thing: keeping your data under your control. Self-hosting removes the risk that your data is sent out, stored elsewhere, or used to train a vendor's model. If your main worry is data leaving the building, self-hosting directly solves it. See [Chapter 8](ch08-self-hosting-keep-your-data-under-control.md).

But "safer" is not total. Self-hosting moves the security job onto you. Now you are responsible for patching, access control, backups, and keeping the machine safe. A badly secured self-hosted system can be less safe than a well-run cloud service. You have traded the vendor's security for your own.

Also, self-hosting does not fix wrong answers. A model on your own machine can still produce a confident mistake. You still need a human check. So self-hosting is the right choice when data control is the priority and you can handle the security work. It is not a magic safety switch. The honest comparison is in [Chapter 8](ch08-self-hosting-keep-your-data-under-control.md) and [Chapter 11](ch11-digital-sovereignty.md).

## What does "trustless" mean, and do I really need it?

"Trustless" does not mean trusting no one or being suspicious. It means a system designed so you do not have to blindly trust the other party — because the design itself protects you. The idea comes from secure systems where you verify, rather than assume. [Chapter 7](ch07-trustless-trust-without-trusting.md) is the canonical home for the concept.

In everyday business terms, a trustless approach means you keep proof, you check rather than assume, and you set things up so a mistake or a dishonest partner cannot silently harm you. For AI, it looks like: keep logs of what the system did, verify outputs instead of trusting them, use tools that let you see and control your data, and avoid setups where you must simply believe the vendor.

Do you "need" it? You need the spirit of it, yes. Blind trust in any tool or vendor is risky. But you do not need to build a fortress. The practical version is simple: verify, keep records, control your data, and keep a human accountable. That is trustless enough for a small business, and it costs very little.
