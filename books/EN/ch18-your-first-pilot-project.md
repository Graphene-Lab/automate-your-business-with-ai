# Chapter 18 — Your First Pilot Project

## In Simple Words

A pilot project is a small, careful test of a new way of working before you commit to it fully. The word "pilot" here means the same as a pilot light — a small flame you light first to make sure the big one will work safely. You try one thing, on a small scale, for a short time, and you learn from it before you go big.

This is the single most important habit in this whole book. Most failed AI projects fail because someone went big too fast — bought a tool, rolled it out everywhere, and discovered too late that it did not fit. A pilot prevents that. It lets you fail small, cheap, and early, which is the best possible way to fail.

Think of it like testing a new recipe for your restaurant. You would not change the menu for every customer on day one. You cook the dish for a few tables, watch who smiles and who pushes the plate away, adjust the seasoning, and only then put it on the full menu. A pilot is that first few tables.

A pilot has four marks: it is **small** (one process, not the whole company), **short** (weeks, not years), **measured** (you know before you start what "success" looks like), and **reversible** (if it fails, you can undo it easily). Keep those four in mind and you will run good pilots.

The goal of your first pilot is not to change the world. It is to learn how to run a project, build confidence in your team, and prove the method on one small win. A small win creates momentum. A big failure creates fear. Choose the small win.

## A Bit of History

**The word itself comes from the sea.** A "pilot" was originally a specialist who boarded a ship to guide it through dangerous, unfamiliar waters — a harbor, a reef, a narrow channel. The pilot did not own the ship or sail the whole voyage. They came aboard for the risky part, used local knowledge to get through safely, and left. That is exactly what a pilot project does for a business: it guides you through the risky part of a change on a small scale before you sail the whole journey.

**Early 1900s: pilots in industry and science.** The idea of testing on a small scale before scaling spread into engineering and science. Before building a whole factory, you build a model. Before rolling out a process everywhere, you try it on one line. "Pilot plant" became standard in manufacturing — a small version of a factory used to work out the kinks.

**Mid-1900s: pilots in policy and medicine.** Governments tested new programs in one town before a national rollout. Medicine tested a treatment on a small group before giving it to everyone. The controlled trial — a small test with a clear measure and a comparison — became the gold standard for honest evidence.

**2000s: the startup "minimum viable product."** Software startups adopted the same logic under a new name. Instead of building a perfect product for years, they built a tiny version, put it in front of real users fast, learned, and improved. The lesson: learn from reality early rather than guess for a long time.

**2020s: pilots become essential for AI.** AI tools are powerful but unpredictable in real work. The gap between a clean demo and messy reality is wide. Running a pilot is no longer optional caution — it is the basic way to find out whether an AI tool actually helps *your* business before you bet the whole operation on it.

The arc: from the harbor to the factory to the lab to the startup. The shape never changed — test small, learn fast, then scale.

## Curiosity

### Why "pilot" is the perfect word

It is worth pausing on the word, because it carries the whole method inside it. A maritime pilot is not the captain. The captain still owns the ship and makes the final calls. The pilot is a temporary expert who boards for the hard part, brings local knowledge, and guides the vessel through the rocks. Then the pilot leaves and the captain sails on.

A good pilot project works the same way. You, the business owner, stay the captain. The pilot is a temporary experiment that helps you get through the uncertain part of a change. You are not handing your company over to AI, and you are not locked in. You are testing the channel with a small, reversible move. If the channel is clear, you sail the whole fleet through. If there are rocks, you found out with one small boat, not the whole fleet.

That framing matters because it lowers the fear. A pilot is not a leap. It is a sounding of the water with a short pole before you step in. The oldest, safest way to cross an unknown stream.

## A Real Business Example

*The following is an illustrative composite of common real-world patterns, not a single named company.*

A regional insurance broker wanted to use AI to answer customer emails faster. The tempting move was to switch it on for all 40 staff and all incoming mail at once. Instead, the owner ran a pilot.

**Small.** Only two staff, only one type of email (address-change requests), for three weeks.

**Measured.** Before starting, they measured the baseline: an address-change email took about 12 minutes to handle. The success target: under 6 minutes, with no errors in the changes made.

**Reversible.** The AI only drafted replies; a human pressed send. If it failed, they simply turned it off and nothing was broken.

In week one, the AI drafted replies that were fast but sometimes too stiff in tone. They adjusted the template. In week two, it handled 90% of the cases well. In week three, the average handling time was 4 minutes, and no address changes were wrong. The pilot passed.

Only then did they roll it out to more email types and more staff — and they ran a small pilot for each new type before trusting it. The whole rollout took three months instead of three weeks, but it worked, and the team trusted it because they had seen it proven step by step.

The lesson: the small pilot was not a delay. It was the reason the big rollout succeeded.

## How to Do It

### 18.1 Choosing a small but useful process

Your first pilot should be chosen for **learning**, not for glory. Pick a process that is small enough to control but useful enough to matter.

Good first-pilot traits:

- **Repetitive.** The same task happens over and over, so you get many chances to learn.
- **Low risk.** If the AI gets it wrong, no one is hurt and nothing is destroyed. Avoid anything involving money movement, legal filings, or health data for a first try.
- **Measurable.** You can count the time, the errors, or the volume, so you can prove change.
- **Boring enough that people welcome help.** Pick a task your team dislikes. They will be allies, not opponents.
- **Self-contained.** It does not depend on ten other things changing at once.

A great first pilot: summarizing incoming emails, drafting standard replies, extracting data from forms, or organizing documents. A bad first pilot: replacing the sales team, or automating payroll. Start where a mistake is cheap and a win is visible.

### 18.2 Drawing the "before" and the "after"

Before you touch anything, draw a clear picture of the process as it is now — the **"before."** Then draw the process as you hope it will be — the **"after."** This pair of pictures is your map and your target.

For the "before," write down every step as it happens today, who does it, and roughly how long each step takes. Be honest. Include the messy parts, the re-checks, the "then I email Dave to ask." This is your baseline.

For the "after," mark which steps the AI will take over, which stay human, and what you expect to change. Keep it realistic. The "after" is not a fantasy; it is a plan you will test.

Put the two side by side. The gap between them is what the pilot is testing. If you cannot draw both pictures, you do not yet understand the process well enough to pilot it. Draw them first.

A simple format:

| Step | Before (who, how long) | After (who, how long) |
|---|---|---|
| Receive request | Staff reads, 2 min | AI reads and sorts, 0 min |
| Draft reply | Staff writes, 8 min | AI drafts, staff checks, 2 min |
| Send | Staff sends, 1 min | Staff sends, 1 min |

The table turns a vague hope into a concrete, checkable plan.

### 18.3 Involving the team

Do not run a pilot from your office and announce it like a decree. The people doing the work every day know things you do not. Involve them from the start, and they become your best source of truth and your strongest supporters.

How to involve them well:

- **Tell them why.** Explain the goal honestly: to remove the boring parts, not to replace them. Fear turns people against a pilot; clarity turns them toward it. (See [Chapter 15](ch15-people-roles-and-culture.md) on people and culture.)
- **Ask them to map the process.** They will spot the edge cases and the hidden steps you missed.
- **Give them the power to say "this is wrong."** Make it safe to report failures. If people hide the AI's mistakes out of fear, you learn nothing.
- **Pick a champion.** One enthusiastic person on the team who helps others and feeds back honestly.
- **Celebrate their role in the win.** When the pilot succeeds, credit the team. It builds trust for the next project.

A pilot done *to* a team fails. A pilot done *with* a team works.

### 18.4 Test, fix, document

A pilot is a loop: test, fix, test again. Expect to fix things. A pilot that goes perfectly on day one is suspicious — you probably did not test hard enough.

**Test.** Run the new process on real work. Watch what happens. Collect the failures as carefully as the successes.

**Fix.** When something goes wrong, adjust — the template, the instructions, the settings, the handoff between AI and human. Then test again.

**Document.** Write down what you learned as you go. What worked, what broke, how you fixed it, what rule you now follow. This written record is gold. It becomes the instruction manual for the full rollout, and the training material for new staff. It also lets you repeat the win deliberately instead of by luck.

Keep a simple pilot log: date, what you tried, what happened, what you changed. By the end, you will have a playbook you did not have before you started.

### 18.5 Success criteria

Decide what "success" means **before** you start, in numbers, not feelings. If you decide after, you will talk yourself into whatever happened.

Good success criteria are specific and measurable:

- **Time.** "Average handling time drops from 12 minutes to under 6."
- **Quality.** "Fewer than 1 error in 50 cases."
- **Adoption.** "At least 80% of eligible cases go through the new process."
- **Satisfaction.** "The pilot team says they would keep it."

Set a **pass line** and a **fail line** for each. If you clear the pass line, the pilot succeeded. If you fall below the fail line, it failed — and that is a fine outcome, because you learned it cheaply. If you land in between, you adjust and run a little longer.

Write the criteria down and share them with the team. Everyone should know what they are aiming for and be able to see whether they hit it.

### 18.6 When to move on to the next project

A pilot ending well is not the finish line; it is a gate. Decide in advance what you do at the gate.

**If the pilot passed:** scale it gradually. Roll it out to more cases or more people, but keep a light check for the first while. Do not switch off all human review at once. Run a small pilot again for each meaningfully new case.

**If the pilot failed:** stop and learn. A failed pilot is not a disaster — it is a cheap lesson that saved you from an expensive mistake. Ask why it failed. Was the tool wrong? The process wrong? The data wrong? Fix the cause and try again, or walk away. Either way, you gained knowledge and lost little.

**If the pilot was mixed:** adjust and re-run. Most pilots land here. Tweak the design and test again.

When to move to the *next* project: when the current one is stable and running without constant firefighting, and you have documented how it works. Do not chase a new shiny thing while the last one is still a mess. Finish, stabilize, document, then move on. Momentum comes from completed projects, not started ones.

## Ethics and Responsibility

A pilot touches real work and often real customer data, so ethics is not a side note.

**Protect data during the pilot.** Even a small test must respect privacy. Do not feed customer data into a tool you have not vetted. Know where the data goes and who can see it (see [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md)).

**Keep a human in the loop where it matters.** For a first pilot, the AI should draft, not act. A human presses send, approves, or decides. This keeps accountability with a person.

**Be honest with your team.** Do not hide the real goal or dress up a job-cutting plan as a "pilot." Trust is hard to build and easy to break. Tell the truth about what you are testing and why.

**Do not let a pilot become a way to avoid responsibility.** If the AI makes a mistake that harms a customer, a person is still accountable. Design the pilot so a human checks the output that matters.

**Respect the people whose work you are changing.** Involve them, listen to them, and credit them. A pilot that steamrolls the team teaches the whole company to fear the next change.

Run the pilot the way you would want it run if you were the one doing the work.

## Mistakes to Avoid

**Going too big too fast.** Rolling out everywhere before testing. This is the classic failure. Start small.

**No baseline.** Not measuring the "before," so you cannot prove the "after." Measure first.

**Vague success criteria.** "It seems better" is not a result. Set numbers before you start.

**Testing only the easy cases.** If you only test clean, simple inputs, you will be surprised by the messy ones. Test the hard cases on purpose.

**Excluding the team.** Running it from above and announcing it. The team will resist what they did not help build.

**Hiding failures.** If people fear reporting mistakes, you learn nothing. Make it safe to report.

**No documentation.** Learning by doing but writing nothing down, so the win cannot be repeated or taught.

**Skipping the human check.** Letting the AI act alone on something that matters. Keep a human on the send button.

**Moving on too soon.** Chasing the next project before the last one is stable and documented.

**Treating a failed pilot as a disaster.** A failed pilot is a cheap lesson. Punishing failure teaches people to hide failure.

**Confusing the pilot with the rollout.** A pilot proves it *can* work; the rollout makes it work *everywhere*. Do not skip steps between them.

## Practical Exercise

### 18.7 Exercise: the pilot plan

Write a one-page pilot plan for one small process in your business. Fill in every line.

**1. The process.** Name the one small, repetitive, low-risk task you will pilot. (Example: drafting replies to address-change emails.)

**2. The "before."** Write the current steps, who does each, and the time each takes. This is your baseline. If you do not know the times, measure them this week first.

**3. The "after."** Write the new steps. Mark which the AI does, which stay human. Keep a human on any step that matters.

**4. The scope.** Who is involved (keep it to one or two people), what cases are included, and how long the pilot runs (two to four weeks).

**5. The success criteria.** Write the pass line and the fail line for time, quality, and adoption. Numbers, not feelings.

**6. The reversibility.** Write exactly how you turn it off and return to the old way if it fails. Confirm nothing breaks.

**7. The team plan.** How you will explain the "why," who your champion is, and how people report problems safely.

**8. The log.** Set up a simple log: date, what you tried, what happened, what you changed.

**9. The gate.** Write what you will do if it passes (scale gradually), fails (stop and learn), or is mixed (adjust and re-run).

Put all nine on one page. That page is your pilot. Run it, fill in the log, and at the end you will have something you did not have before: proof, a playbook, and the confidence to do the next one.

## Checklist

### 18.8 Pilot checklist

Before you start a pilot, and before you finish it, check every box.

- [ ] **The process is small, repetitive, low-risk, and measurable.**
- [ ] **I have drawn the "before" with real times (the baseline).**
- [ ] **I have drawn the "after" with AI and human steps marked.**
- [ ] **A human stays in the loop on any step that matters.**
- [ ] **The scope is small: one or two people, one case type.**
- [ ] **The pilot has a fixed, short time frame (2–4 weeks).**
- [ ] **I set success criteria as numbers, with a pass line and a fail line.**
- [ ] **I shared the success criteria with the team.**
- [ ] **I explained the "why" honestly to the team.**
- [ ] **I have a champion on the team.**
- [ ] **It is safe for the team to report failures.**
- [ ] **I have a clear way to reverse the pilot if it fails.**
- [ ] **I am testing the messy/hard cases, not only the easy ones.**
- [ ] **I am keeping a pilot log (what tried, what happened, what changed).**
- [ ] **I documented the playbook so the win can be repeated and taught.**
- [ ] **I decided in advance what to do at the gate: pass, fail, or mixed.**

If a box is empty, the pilot is not ready. Fill it first. A pilot you run well is worth more than a rollout you hope will work.

## Key Takeaways

- A pilot is a small, short, measured, reversible test — the best way to fail is small and early, before you bet the whole business.
- Measure the "before" and draw the "after" before you start; without a baseline, you can never prove the change.
- Involve the team and make it safe to report failures — a pilot done *with* people works, one done *to* them fails.
- Set success criteria as numbers with a pass line and a fail line before you begin, so you cannot talk yourself into a result.
- Finish, stabilize, and document before chasing the next project — momentum comes from completed pilots, not started ones.
