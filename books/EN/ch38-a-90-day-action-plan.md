# Chapter 38 — A 90-Day Action Plan

## In Simple Words

A plan you never start is a wish. This chapter turns intention into a schedule you can begin on a Monday and finish in about three months. Ninety days is the unit: long enough to learn something real and see a result, short enough to keep the urgency alive.

The plan is a pilot with a clock on it. A pilot is a small, careful test before you commit fully — the method explained in [Chapter 18 — Your First Pilot Project](ch18-your-first-pilot-project.md). Here that method is laid out week by week so you always know what to do next and when.

The shape is four phases. First you **look** at how work really happens. Then you **choose** one small thing to test and decide what success means. Then you **run** the test and train the people. Then you **measure** what happened and make a clear decision: keep it, fix it, or drop it.

Nothing here needs a big budget or a technical team. It needs your attention, one small project, and honesty about the numbers. By day 90 you will have one of three good outcomes: a thing that works and is worth spreading, a thing you learned enough about to fix, or a thing you learned is not worth doing — which saves you from a costly mistake. All three are wins compared with guessing.

Keep one rule in mind throughout: **go small.** The most common way this plan fails is starting too big. One process, a handful of people, a short test. You can always go bigger later. You cannot un-spend a fortune on a project that was too large to fail cheaply.

## A Bit of History

The idea of a deliberate first ninety days is borrowed from good management practice.

**The "first 90 days" of a new leader.** Management thinking long held that a person taking a new role has a short window to understand the situation and set direction before old habits harden. The popular framing is that the first ninety days decide much of what follows. The insight is simple: a fixed early window forces you to learn fast and act before drift sets in.

**The two-week sprint.** In software, teams moved from year-long projects to short cycles called sprints — usually one to four weeks — where they build a little, show it, learn, and adjust. The lesson: short cycles with a real review beat long plans that never meet reality.

**The season as a unit.** Ninety days is roughly a season. It is long enough to plant, tend, and harvest something, and short enough to feel the whole arc. That makes it a natural planning rhythm for a small business that is not going to wait two years to know if something worked.

**Why ninety days fits AI.** AI tools are powerful but unpredictable in real work, so the gap between a clean demo and a messy Monday is wide. A fixed ninety-day window forces you to close that gap with evidence, not hope. You start small, you measure honestly, and you decide on a date instead of drifting.

The thread across all of these is the same: a short, fixed window with a clear review at the end is the cheapest way to learn the truth. That is what this plan gives you.

## Curiosity

### The "21 days to form a habit" is a myth — and why that helps you

You have probably heard that a new habit takes twenty-one days. It is a comforting number. It is also wrong.

The 21-day idea came from a plastic surgeon's observation in the 1960s, not from careful study. When researchers actually measured how long habits take, the picture was different. A well-known 2009 study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues found that, on average, a habit took around two months to become automatic — and the range was wide, from a few weeks to many months. Some things stuck fast; others took a long time.

Why this is good news for your plan: if you had aimed at 21 days, you would have quit just as the real work of building a habit was beginning. A 90-day window sits comfortably past the average. It gives a new routine — checking AI output, using a new tool, reviewing a metric — enough time to actually settle in before you judge whether it stuck.

So when you build your ninety-day plan, do not expect the new way to feel natural in three weeks. Expect it to feel awkward for a while and to click later. Plan for the real curve, not the myth, and you will not give up too early.

## A Real Business Example

### A 90-day arc in a small service firm (illustrative)

This example is illustrative — a composite picture of how the plan plays out, not a single reported company. Use it to see the shape, not as a promise of results.

A twelve-person accounting practice wanted to spend less time answering the same client questions. Over ninety days they ran the four phases. In weeks one and two they watched and wrote down every repeated question for a fortnight. In weeks three and four they chose one pilot — a first-draft reply assistant for the ten most common questions — and set a target: cut the time spent on those replies by half, with the accountant still reviewing every answer. In weeks five to eight they set up the tool, trained three staff, and ran it on real emails with a human check on everything. In weeks nine to twelve they measured: the time on those ten questions fell, some drafts needed heavy edits, and two question types the tool handled poorly were pulled back to humans.

At day 90 they decided to keep the pilot for eight of the ten question types, fix the workflow for the other two, and plan a second pilot for a different task. Nobody was fired. The accountants spent the saved hours on advisory work they billed at a higher rate. The whole thing cost a few hundred euros in tool fees and a manager's focused attention. That is the realistic shape: a small, measured, reversible win that teaches you how to run the next one.

## How to Do It

Run the four phases in order. Each has a goal, a short list of actions, and a deliverable you can point to at the end. Do not skip ahead.

### Week 1–2: Observe and map

**Goal:** see how the work actually happens, not how the org chart says it happens.

Spend two weeks watching one area. Do not change anything yet. Your job is to notice.

- **Pick one area to observe.** Choose a process that repeats and annoys you — client questions, invoice handling, report writing, scheduling.
- **List the repeated tasks.** Write down what people do over and over, and roughly how long each takes.
- **Find the pain and the volume.** Note which tasks are most repetitive, most time-consuming, or most error-prone. High volume plus high pain is where a pilot pays.
- **Spot the data.** Note what information the task uses and where it lives. A task with no readable data is hard to automate. [Chapter 14](ch14-data-the-raw-material.md) helps here.
- **Talk to the people doing it.** Ask what they wish they did not have to do. They know the real shape of the work.

**Deliverable:** a one-page map of the area — the repeated tasks, their rough time cost, and the top two or three pain points.

### Week 3–4: Choose the pilot and the objectives

**Goal:** pick one small test and define, in numbers, what success means.

- **Choose one process, not several.** Use the map from weeks one and two. Pick the single task with the best mix of volume and pain that is reversible. The impact-and-ease thinking in [Chapter 12](ch12-where-ai-can-help-your-business.md) helps you rank options.
- **Make it small and reversible.** One task, a few people, a short run. If it fails, you can undo it in a day.
- **Set a baseline first.** Measure the current number before you change anything — minutes per task, replies per day, errors per week. Without a baseline you cannot prove anything later.
- **Write one clear objective.** For example: "cut time on the ten common client questions by 40%, with a human reviewing every reply." One number, one guardrail.
- **Name an owner.** One person is responsible for the pilot. No owner, no pilot.
- **Set the guardrails.** Decide what the AI may and may not do, and what always needs a human. Money, legal, and personal-data steps stay human-checked. See [Chapter 20](ch20-implementing-ai-securely.md).

**Deliverable:** a one-page pilot brief — the chosen task, the baseline number, the objective, the owner, and the guardrails.

### Week 5–8: Test tools and train people

**Goal:** run the pilot on real work, with people trained and watching.

- **Pick the tool last, not first.** Choose a tool that fits the task you already defined. Do not let a shiny tool pick your project. [Chapter 17](ch17-choosing-tools-without-being-fooled.md) covers how to choose without being fooled.
- **Start with a human check on everything.** For the first weeks, a person reviews every AI output before it goes out. This is non-negotiable while you learn.
- **Train the people who use it.** Teach them to ask clearly, to check the answer, and to know when to override. A few hours of hands-on practice beats a manual.
- **Run on real work, small volume.** Use real cases, not toy examples, but keep the volume low so a mistake is cheap.
- **Keep a daily log.** Note what worked, what failed, and what needed fixing. This log is gold for the decision at the end.
- **Adjust as you go.** Expect to tune prompts, change a step, or drop a case type. That is the pilot doing its job.

**Deliverable:** a running pilot with a daily log and a trained user or two.

### Week 9–12: Measure and decide

**Goal:** compare the result to the baseline and make a clear call.

- **Measure against the baseline.** Re-measure the same number you took in weeks three and four. Did it move? By how much?
- **Check quality, not just speed.** Faster is worthless if the output is wrong. Count errors, edits, and complaints.
- **Ask the users.** Did it help or get in the way? The people running it know.
- **Make one of three decisions.** **Keep and spread** it to more of the work. **Fix and re-test** the parts that fell short. **Drop it** if it does not pay. All three are fine; guessing wrong cheaply is the point.
- **Write the decision down.** One paragraph: what happened, what you decided, what is next. This becomes the start of your second pilot.
- **Decide the next step.** If it worked, plan the rollout or the next pilot. If it failed, name why and pick a better target.

**Deliverable:** a short decision memo with the before-and-after numbers and the next step.

## Ethics and Responsibility

A fast plan can do harm fast if you are careless. Build the guardrails in from week one.

**Be honest with your people from the start.** Tell staff what you are testing and why. Do not run a pilot on their work in secret. Fear grows in silence and turns a useful test into a threat. [Chapter 21](ch21-managing-change-in-your-company.md) covers how to bring people along.

**Protect personal data during the test.** Before you feed any customer or employee data into a tool, know where it goes and whether that is allowed. If personal data is involved, follow the rules in [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md) and the security steps in [Chapter 20](ch20-implementing-ai-securely.md).

**Keep a human in the loop for anything that matters.** During the pilot, no AI output reaches a customer, a payment, or a legal decision without a person approving it. This is the core safety rule.

**Do not use the pilot to quietly cut people.** A pilot is for learning, not for building a case to remove staff. If the test shows a task can be automated, plan what the person does next. The ethical duty is covered in [Chapter 4](ch04-ethical-ai-doing-the-right-thing.md).

**Be transparent with customers if they are involved.** If a customer sees AI-assisted output, make clear what is automated and give a route to a human.

## Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Skipping the mapping.** If you jump straight to a tool, you pick the wrong target. Two weeks of watching is cheap insurance.

**Mistake 2: Choosing too big a pilot.** A pilot that touches the whole company cannot fail cheaply. One task, a few people.

**Mistake 3: No baseline.** If you did not measure before, you cannot prove the change helped. Take the number first.

**Mistake 4: No owner.** A pilot with no single responsible person drifts and quietly dies. Name one.

**Mistake 5: Training too late.** Train before go-live, not after people are confused and frustrated.

**Mistake 6: No human check early on.** Letting unchecked AI output reach customers during a test is how a pilot becomes a PR problem.

**Mistake 7: Not deciding at day 90.** A pilot that never ends is just an expensive habit. Make the keep / fix / drop call on the date.

**Mistake 8: Scope creep.** Adding "one more thing" mid-pilot breaks the measurement. Finish the test, then expand.

## Practical Exercise

### Lay out your own 90-day calendar

Take a sheet or a simple calendar and block the four phases with real dates.

**Step 1: Set day one.** Pick the Monday you will start. Mark day 90.

**Step 2: Block the phases.** Weeks 1–2 observe, weeks 3–4 choose, weeks 5–8 run, weeks 9–12 measure. Put the four phase names on the calendar.

**Step 3: Name the deliverables.** Beside each phase, write the one-page output you must have at the end: the map, the pilot brief, the running log, the decision memo.

**Step 4: Set three checkpoints.** A review at the end of week 4 (is the pilot chosen?), week 8 (is it running?), and week 12 (the decision). Put them in the calendar with yourself.

**Step 5: Write your one objective now.** Before you start, draft the single objective sentence you hope to prove. You will refine it in week three, but writing it early focuses the whole plan.

Keep the calendar visible. The fixed dates are what turn a good intention into a finished test.

## Key Takeaways

- Ninety days is long enough to learn and short enough to stay urgent — a pilot with a clock on it.
- Four phases: observe and map, choose and set objectives, run and train, measure and decide.
- Always take a baseline before you change anything, or you cannot prove the result.
- Pick the tool last, keep the pilot small and reversible, and name one owner.
- Keep a human check on every output during the test; never let unchecked AI reach a customer or a payment.
- End on the date with a clear keep / fix / drop decision — all three are good outcomes.
- Be honest with staff and customers, and protect personal data throughout.
- The plan's real product is not one automation; it is the skill to run the next one.
