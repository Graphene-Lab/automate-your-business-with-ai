# Chapter 17 — Choosing Tools Without Being Fooled

## In Simple Words

Every AI vendor will tell you their tool is easy, cheap, and revolutionary. Some of them are telling the truth. Many are not. This chapter is your defense. It teaches you how to look past the shiny demo and the confident salesperson, and choose a tool that actually fits your business.

The most important idea here is simple: **you are not buying a tool, you are buying a result.** A tool is only worth what it does for you. A beautiful product that does not solve your problem is an expensive toy. Your job is to connect every choice back to a real problem and a real number.

A good analogy is buying a car. The showroom makes every car look amazing. The leather shines, the engine purrs, the salesperson is charming. But you do not buy a car for the showroom. You buy it for the road you actually drive, the passengers you actually carry, and the fuel you can afford. So you ask hard questions, you take it for a test drive on *your* roads, and you read the running costs. Choosing AI tools is the same discipline.

This chapter covers how to pick between buying ready-made software, building something custom, or doing it yourself. It gives you the exact questions to ask a vendor. It shows you where hidden costs hide. It explains why a demo is not enough and how to run a small pilot instead. And it looks at open-source tools — software whose code is free to use and change — as a real, affordable option.

One rule to carry with you: **slow down.** The salesperson wants urgency. "This price ends Friday." "Only two licenses left." Urgency is a trick to stop you thinking. A good tool survives a week of careful thought. A bad one relies on you not thinking. Take the week.

## A Bit of History

**1960s–1970s: software is custom.** In the early days, if a company wanted software, it paid specialists to write it from scratch. There was no "off-the-shelf" option. Software was a bespoke suit, made to measure, expensive, and slow to deliver.

**1980s: the packaged software revolution.** Products like spreadsheets and word processors arrived in a box. For the first time, a business could buy a general tool and adapt it to many jobs. This was cheaper and faster than custom builds. The modern software market was born.

**1990s–2000s: big suites and the lock-in trap.** Large vendors sold huge integrated suites — one product for everything. They worked well, but switching away was painful and costly. Companies discovered that the easy choice today could become an expensive prison tomorrow. The word **vendor lock-in** — being stuck with a supplier because leaving is too hard — entered the business vocabulary. (Lock-in is covered fully in [Chapter 9](ch09-third-party-services-and-shadow-ai.md).)

**2000s: open-source goes mainstream.** Linux, Apache, and later thousands of free projects proved that software could be built by communities and given away, yet power the world's biggest websites. Open-source stopped being a hobby and became a serious, trusted option.

**2010s: the subscription era.** Software moved from "buy once" to "pay every month." This lowered the entry price but added a new kind of cost that never ends. Subscriptions made it easy to start and easy to lose track of what you were paying for.

**2020s: the AI gold rush.** Hundreds of AI tools appeared almost overnight, many with big funding and bigger promises. The gap between what a demo shows and what a tool delivers in real work has never been wider. Choosing well is now a core business skill, not a nice extra.

The arc: from bespoke, to boxed, to subscribed, to flooded. The tools keep changing. The need to choose carefully never does.

## Curiosity

### 17.6 The book "Headcount Zero" — a company with no employees

How far can the "no people needed" idea go? There is a book that pushes it to the extreme. It is called **"Headcount Zero: How to Build an AI-Run Company with Paperclip"**, by **Anthony David Adams**. It is published as an open-source book on GitHub (the repository `AnthonyDavidAdams/zero-employee-company-book`), so anyone can read it for free.

The question the book asks is: *what if you never had to hire anyone?* Instead of employees, the founder runs a company made of **AI agents** — software programs that can carry out tasks on their own. The founder becomes a kind of manager of an org chart full of AI workers, coordinated by an open-source platform the book calls **Paperclip**.

This is not a plan most small businesses should follow tomorrow. A company with literally zero humans is an extreme thought experiment, and it has obvious limits: who is accountable when something goes wrong? Who holds the legal responsibility? Who understands a customer's unspoken need? (These are exactly the human roles discussed in [Chapter 15](ch15-people-roles-and-culture.md).)

But as a lens, "Headcount Zero" is useful. It forces one honest question: **how much of your work is truly human, and how much is routine that a machine could carry?** Most businesses find that a surprising share of daily tasks is routine. That does not mean firing everyone. It means freeing people from the boring parts so they can do the judgment, the relationships, and the creative work that machines cannot. Read the book as a provocation, not an instruction. It shows you the edge of the possible, and the edge is where the interesting questions live.

## A Real Business Example

*The following is an illustrative composite of common real-world patterns, not a single named company.*

A small accounting firm wanted to "automate with AI." A vendor gave a dazzling demo: the tool read a stack of receipts and produced a tidy summary in seconds. The owner signed a two-year contract on the spot, dazzled.

Six months later, reality looked different. The demo had used a handful of clean, perfect receipts. The firm's real receipts were crumpled, blurry, in three languages, and full of edge cases. The tool got them wrong often enough that staff had to re-check everything, which meant the "saving" was tiny. The two-year contract locked them in. The subscription kept billing. The tool became an expensive ornament.

A second firm chose differently. Before signing anything, it asked the vendor for a **trial on its own messy data**, not the vendor's clean demo set. It ran a two-week pilot on real receipts. The tool handled about 70% well and failed on the rest. That number — 70% — let the firm make a clear decision: keep the tool for the easy 70%, keep humans on the hard 30%, and sign only a one-year contract with a clear exit. The same tool, chosen with discipline instead of dazzle, became a genuine help.

The difference was not the software. It was that the second firm refused to be fooled by a demo and tested on its own reality first.

## How to Do It

### 17.1 Off-the-shelf software, custom-built, or do-it-yourself

When you need a tool, you have three broad paths. Each has a place.

**Off-the-shelf (buy it ready-made).** This is software you subscribe to or purchase as-is, like a chatbot service or a document-summarizing app. It is the fastest and usually the cheapest way to start. You get it today, it works out of the box, and someone else maintains it. The trade-off: it does what *it* was built to do, not exactly what *you* wanted. If your need is common, an off-the-shelf tool is almost always the right first choice.

**Custom-built (pay someone to build it).** A developer writes software just for you. This fits your needs perfectly. But it is slow, expensive, and you now own a thing that needs maintenance forever. Custom builds make sense only when your need is special and no ready-made tool covers it — and when the value is high enough to justify the cost. For most small businesses, custom-built is rarely the first move.

**Do-it-yourself (you build it, with no-code or light tools).** Tools like automation apps let you connect services yourself without writing code. This is cheap and flexible, and you learn a lot. The trade-off: it takes your time, and if you leave, the thing you built can be hard for someone else to maintain. DIY is great for small automations you can own and keep simple.

A simple rule of thumb: **start off-the-shelf. Go DIY for small glue-work between tools. Go custom only when nothing else fits and the prize is big.** Most businesses live happily on the first two.

### 17.2 Questions to ask the vendor

Never take a vendor's word. Ask direct questions and listen carefully to the answers — especially to what they dodge. Keep these ready:

- **What exactly does this do, and what does it NOT do?** Force them past the marketing. A confident "it handles everything" is a red flag.
- **What does it look like when it fails?** Every tool fails somewhere. An honest vendor can name its weak spots. A vendor who says "it never fails" is lying or clueless.
- **What is the total cost over three years, not just the first month?** Make them say the full number out loud.
- **What happens to my data?** Where is it stored, who can see it, and do you train your AI on it? (This matters for privacy — see [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md).)
- **Can I get my data out, and in what format?** This is your escape hatch. If you cannot leave cleanly, you are locked in.
- **What support do I get, and how fast?** Response times, channels, and whether support is included or costs extra.
- **Who else uses this in my industry, and can I talk to them?** A real reference is worth ten demos.
- **What is your roadmap, and how stable is the company?** A tool from a shaky startup may vanish. Ask how long they have been in business and who funds them.
- **What is the contract, and how do I cancel?** Read the exit terms before you sign, not after.

Write down the answers. Compare vendors on the same questions. The vendor who answers clearly and honestly stands out from the one who waves and charms.

### 17.3 Hidden costs and subscriptions

The sticker price is the smallest cost. The real cost hides in places most people never look. (The full cost method lives in [Chapter 16](ch16-goals-costs-and-return-on-investment.md); here is the vendor-specific version.)

Watch for these:

- **Per-seat pricing.** Many tools charge per user. A "cheap" tool becomes expensive when you add your whole team. Count seats before you sign.
- **Usage-based fees.** Some AI tools charge per task, per message, or per document. A busy month can produce a surprise bill. Ask exactly how usage is metered.
- **Setup and onboarding fees.** Getting started can cost extra, sometimes more than the first year of subscription.
- **Integration costs.** Connecting the tool to your existing systems may need paid help.
- **Premium features behind a paywall.** The feature that sold you may be on a higher tier. Check which tier you actually need.
- **Training and support as add-ons.** "Support included" often means a help article, not a person. Real help may cost more.
- **The never-ending subscription.** A monthly fee looks small but never stops. Multiply it by three or five years to see the true weight.
- **Exit and switching costs.** Canceling may be hard, and moving your data elsewhere may take paid work.

Before you sign, build a **three-year total cost** for each option. Add every one of these. The vendor's monthly price is often only a third of the real three-year number.

### 17.4 Demos and pilot projects

A demo is a performance. It shows the tool at its best, on data chosen to win you over. Never decide on a demo alone.

A **pilot project** is the honest alternative. A pilot is a small, time-boxed trial of the tool on *your* real work, with *your* real messy data, measuring a *your* real result. Where a demo shows you a highlight reel, a pilot shows you the truth.

How to run a good pilot:

- **Use your own data, including the messy cases.** Do not accept the vendor's clean sample.
- **Keep it small and short.** Two to four weeks on one process is enough to learn a lot.
- **Define success before you start.** What number must the tool hit to count as a pass? (This links to the pilot method in [Chapter 18](ch18-your-first-pilot-project.md).)
- **Test the failure, not just the success.** Push it with hard cases on purpose.
- **Try the support.** Send a support request during the pilot and see how fast and helpful they are.
- **Test the exit.** Try exporting your data. Make sure you can leave.

A vendor who refuses a real pilot on your data is telling you something. A vendor who welcomes it is confident for good reason.

### 17.5 Open-source tools: an affordable and flexible alternative

**Open-source** means the software's source code — the instructions that make it run — is free for anyone to see, use, and change. You are not "pirating" it; open-source is a legal, common, and often excellent way software is made. The web you are using right now likely runs on open-source software.

Why consider it for AI:

- **Low or no license cost.** Many open-source tools are free to use.
- **No lock-in.** Because you can see and change the code, you are not trapped with one vendor.
- **You can run it yourself.** Open-source AI models can run on your own machines, which keeps your data under your control (this is **self-hosting**, covered in [Chapter 8](ch08-self-hosting-keep-your-data-under-control.md)).
- **A community behind it.** Popular projects improve fast and have many users to learn from.

The trade-offs:

- **You may need more skill to set it up.** Open-source often assumes you can configure things, or can hire someone who can.
- **Support is community-based.** There may be no one to call. You rely on documentation and forums.
- **You own the maintenance.** If you run it yourself, keeping it updated and secure is on you.

The honest balance: open-source is a powerful, affordable path, especially when data control matters. But it trades money for effort and skill. If you have neither, a paid off-the-shelf tool may be the wiser start. If you have a little technical help, open-source can save you a great deal and free you from lock-in.

## Ethics and Responsibility

Choosing tools is not ethically neutral. Your choices affect your customers, your staff, and your data.

**Protect your customers' data in the choice itself.** Before any tool touches customer data, know where it goes and who can see it. A tool that trains its AI on your customers' private information without consent can break the law (see [Chapter 10](ch10-privacy-and-gdpr.md)).

**Do not let a vendor's hype drive a decision that affects people.** If a tool will change your staff's jobs, choose it for honest reasons and involve your people, not because a salesperson created false urgency.

**Prefer tools you can audit and leave.** A tool that hides how it works, or traps your data, is a poor ethical choice as well as a poor business one. Transparency and a clean exit are signs of a vendor who respects you.

**Be honest in your own claims.** If you buy an AI tool and tell customers "our service uses AI," make sure that is true and not exaggerated marketing.

**Consider the vendor's own ethics.** Where does their data live? Do they respect privacy laws? Are they stable and honest? You are partnering with them; choose a partner you would trust with your name.

A tool is a relationship. Choose the partner as carefully as you would choose a business partner.

## Mistakes to Avoid

### 17.7 Common mistakes

**Buying on the demo.** Deciding from a polished performance on clean data. Always pilot on your own messy reality.

**Ignoring the three-year cost.** Looking at the monthly price and not the total. The subscription never stops.

**Falling for urgency.** "The price ends Friday" is a sales trick. A good tool survives a week of thought.

**No exit plan.** Signing without checking how to get your data out. That is how lock-in happens.

**Buying before defining the problem.** Getting a tool and then looking for a use. Define the problem first, then find the tool.

**Confusing popularity with fit.** A tool used by thousands may still not fit *your* workflow.

**Skipping the support test.** Not checking how the vendor behaves after the sale. Test support during the pilot.

**Over-customizing too early.** Paying for a custom build when an off-the-shelf tool would cover 80% of the need.

**Underestimating DIY maintenance.** Building it yourself and forgetting that you now have to keep it alive.

**Trusting "it handles everything."** No tool does. A vendor who claims otherwise is not telling you the truth.

**Not reading the contract.** Signing without reading the cancellation and data terms. Read before, not after.

**Choosing the cheapest sticker.** The lowest monthly price can hide the highest total cost.

## Practical Exercise

### 17.8 Exercise: an evaluation grid

Build a simple scoring grid to compare tools side by side. This turns a fuzzy choice into a clear one.

**Step 1 — List your options.** Write down two or three candidate tools (or paths: off-the-shelf, DIY, open-source).

**Step 2 — List the criteria.** Use these, or add your own:

- Fits my real problem (0–5)
- Handles my messy data well (0–5)
- Three-year total cost (lower is better — score it)
- Ease of use for my staff (0–5)
- Data privacy and control (0–5)
- Ease of exit / no lock-in (0–5)
- Support quality (0–5)
- Vendor stability (0–5)

**Step 3 — Score each tool.** Give a number for each criterion. Be honest, not hopeful.

**Step 4 — Weight what matters most.** If data privacy is critical to you, double its weight. If cost matters most, weight it higher.

**Step 5 — Add it up.** The tool with the highest weighted score is your front-runner.

**Step 6 — Test the front-runner with a pilot.** The grid narrows the field; the pilot confirms it. Do not skip the pilot.

Put the grid on one page. It makes the decision visible and defensible, and it stops a charming salesperson from overriding your judgment with charm.

## Checklist

### 17.9 Checklist to evaluate a vendor

Before signing with any AI vendor, check every box.

- [ ] **I can state the exact problem this tool solves for me.**
- [ ] **I know what the tool does NOT do, and where it fails.**
- [ ] **I have a three-year total cost, not just the monthly price.**
- [ ] **I know how pricing works** — per seat, per use, tiers, setup fees.
- [ ] **I know where my data is stored and who can see it.**
- [ ] **I know whether the vendor trains its AI on my data, and I consent or refuse.**
- [ ] **I have tested the tool on my own messy data, not the vendor's demo set.**
- [ ] **I have run a small pilot with a defined success number.**
- [ ] **I have tested the vendor's support during the trial.**
- [ ] **I have tested exporting my data out of the tool.**
- [ ] **I have read the cancellation terms before signing.**
- [ ] **I have a reference from another business in my field, or tried to get one.**
- [ ] **I checked the vendor's stability and how long they have been in business.**
- [ ] **I resisted the urgency and took time to think.**
- [ ] **I compared at least two options on the same criteria.**

If a box is empty, you have not finished evaluating. Fill it before you sign. A tool chosen with a clear head is worth far more than one bought in a dazzle.

## Key Takeaways

- You are not buying a tool, you are buying a result — connect every choice back to a real problem and a real number.
- A demo is a performance on clean data; a pilot on your own messy data is the only honest test.
- The monthly price is only a fraction of the cost — always build a three-year total that includes seats, usage, setup, support, and exit.
- Open-source is a real, affordable, lock-in-free path, but it trades money for effort and skill; choose it when you have the help to run it.
- Refuse urgency: a good tool survives a week of careful thought, and a vendor who welcomes a real pilot is confident for good reason.
