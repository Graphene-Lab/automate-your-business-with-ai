# Chapter 21 — Managing Change in Your Company

## In Simple Words

Buying an AI tool is the easy part. Getting your company to actually use it, and to keep using it well, is the hard part. That hard part has a name: **change management**. It means helping people move from how they work today to how they will work tomorrow, without losing their trust, their skills, or their motivation along the way.

Think of it this way. You can install the best tool in the world on every desk by Monday morning. If your people do not understand why it is there, do not know how to use it, or fear what it means for them, they will find ways to avoid it. They will keep doing things the old way and pretend. The tool will sit there, paid for and unused. The technology did not fail. The change did.

This chapter is about the process of change itself: how to talk about it honestly, how to train people properly, how to handle fear and resistance, how to build new habits that stick, and how to keep supporting everyone after the first excitement fades. The people, roles, and culture that this process rests on are covered in [Chapter 15 — People, Roles, and Culture](ch15-people-roles-and-culture.md). Read that chapter for *who* is involved. This one is about *how* the move actually happens over weeks and months.

One idea to carry with you: **people do not resist change; they resist being changed.** When people are dragged somewhere against their will, they push back. When they are told the truth, asked for help, and supported through the awkward middle, most of them will walk with you gladly. Your job as a leader is not to force the walk. It is to make walking with you the natural, safe, supported choice.

Change is not a single event. It is a curve. There is a dip — a period where things feel harder and slower before they feel better. Almost every team hits it. Knowing the dip is coming, and planning for it, is the difference between a change that sticks and one that quietly collapses in month three.

## A Bit of History

**1930s–1940s: the human side of work is discovered.** Earlier in this book we met the Hawthorne studies, where workers produced more when someone paid attention to them. That was the first hard evidence that feelings and involvement, not just pay and process, drive performance. It planted the seed that managing work means managing people, not only tasks.

**1950s–1960s: change becomes a thing you can study.** Social psychologist Kurt Lewin described change in three simple steps that are still taught today. First, **unfreeze**: loosen the old habit so people are ready to move. Second, **change**: introduce the new way. Third, **refreeze**: make the new way the stable, normal way. His insight was that the hardest part is not the middle — it is the first step (getting people off the old habit) and the last (making the new one stick). Most failed changes skip one of these.

**1969: the implementation dip is named.** Education researcher David Fuller, and later others studying how schools adopt new methods, noticed a pattern: when people start something new, their performance drops before it rises. The new way is clumsy at first, so people do it worse than the old way they had mastered. If leaders do not expect the dip, they panic and abandon the change right at the bottom. If they expect it and hold steady, performance climbs past the old level. This is one of the most useful facts in all of change management.

**1990s: the change-management industry is born.** As companies adopted computers, enterprise software, and reorganizations at scale, a whole field grew up around adoption. John Kotter published his famous eight-step model in 1996, stressing that a change needs a sense of urgency, a guiding team, a clear vision, and short-term wins. The ADKAR model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) came from the same era. The common thread: adoption is not luck. It is a sequence you can plan.

**2000s: the graveyard of big programs.** Studies kept finding that a large share of big change programs missed their goals. A widely repeated figure says around 70% fail. The exact number is debated and its origin is fuzzy, so treat it as a rough signal, not a precise fact. The signal is real: big, top-down, poorly-supported change fails often, and the cause is almost always people, not the plan on paper.

**2020s: AI makes change personal and fast.** Earlier software changed how you typed or filed. AI changes what the job *is* — it can write, summarize, and decide. That touches people's identity and their sense of security. And it arrives faster than past waves, so companies have less time to prepare. The old lessons now matter more, not less: tell the truth, involve people, train them, expect the dip, and stay present after launch.

The arc is clear. For eighty years, every wave of new working tools taught the same lesson. The tool is never the change. The people are. AI is the newest and fastest teacher of a very old lesson.

## Curiosity

### Why we cling to the old way: the endowment effect

Here is a small experiment that explains a great deal of workplace resistance. In the 1980s, economists Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler ran a simple study. Half the people in a room were given a coffee mug. Then they were asked how much they would sell the mug for. The other half were asked how much they would pay to buy one. The owners wanted roughly twice as much as the buyers were willing to pay. The mug was identical. The only difference was ownership.

This is called the **endowment effect**: once something is ours, we value it more than an identical thing we do not own. It is not irrational greed; it is how human attachment works. We merge our possessions, our routines, and our skills into our sense of self.

Now apply it to work. Your team's old process — the messy spreadsheet, the way Maria always handles the monthly report, the shortcut everyone learned years ago — is *theirs*. They are endowed in it. They value it more than you do, because you see a process and they see their own competence, their history, their identity. Asking them to give it up for a new AI tool feels, to them, like being asked to give up a part of themselves for free.

This is why "it is obviously better, just use it" does not work. To the person endowed in the old way, it is not obviously better, and the loss feels real. Good change management works *with* the endowment effect, not against it: you honor the old way ("this served us well, and your skill in it is why we can move now"), you let people keep what is valuable to them (their judgment, their role, their pride), and you frame the new tool as adding to them rather than replacing them. When you understand the mug, you understand the resistance.

## A Real Business Example

*The following is an illustrative composite of common real-world patterns, not a single named company.*

Two accounting firms of about fifteen people each adopted the same AI tool to draft client emails and summarize documents.

The first firm launched it like a product release. On a Monday, the owner sent an email: "We have bought an AI assistant. Log in, use it for drafts, it will save time." No meeting. No training beyond a shared link. No mention of what it meant for anyone's job. By Wednesday, two senior staff were openly skeptical. By the end of the month, almost everyone had quietly gone back to writing from scratch and only used the tool when asked. Nobody said so directly, but the fear was clear: "They are teaching the machine to replace us." Six months later the subscription was cancelled. The tool worked fine. The change failed.

The second firm launched it like a journey. Before anything switched on, the owner held a one-hour meeting and said three things plainly. First, the why: "We spend too many hours on repetitive writing, and it is burning people out. This tool takes the boring part so you can spend time on the client work you were trained for." Second, the honest promise: "No one will be let go because of this tool. If anything, it frees us to take on more clients and grow." Third, the ask: "I need your help. You know our clients better than any machine. Try it, break it, tell me what is wrong, and we will fix it together."

They picked two respected staff as "champions" to try it first and answer questions from peers. They ran a hands-on training where everyone practiced on real (anonymized) work. They set one firm rule: the AI drafts, a person reviews and sends — nothing leaves without a human eye. They shared progress every week, including the mistakes and the fixes. When the dip came in week two — when everyone was slower and grumpier — the owner said, "This is the dip, it is normal, keep going," and the team laughed and held steady. By month three, the tool was simply how they worked, and nobody remembered the old way fondly.

Same tool. Opposite outcomes. The difference was never the software. It was the change plan.

## How to Do It

### 21.1 Communicating in a transparent way

Communication is not one email at the start. It is a steady stream of honest talk that runs before, during, and after the change. Get this wrong and every other effort leaks. Get it right and you can survive almost any stumble, because trust holds the team together.

**Lead with the why, not the what.** People tolerate a hard change if they understand the reason. They resent a change that feels arbitrary or hidden. So start with the reason in plain words: what problem are we solving, and why does it matter to us and to our customers? "We are adopting AI" is a *what* and it means nothing. "We spend forty hours a week on repetitive writing that makes people stay late and drains their energy; this tool takes that away" is a *why* you can rally behind.

**Say the quiet part out loud.** The first thing everyone is thinking is, "Will I lose my job?" If you do not answer it, they will answer it themselves, and they will assume the worst. Be honest. If the plan is to redeploy freed time into growth and better work, say exactly that. If some roles will genuinely change, say that too, and say how you will handle it fairly. Do not promise "nothing will change" — that is a lie, and when reality contradicts it later, you lose all trust. The truth, delivered early and kindly, is always safer than a comforting lie that unravels.

**Repeat, repeat, repeat.** What you say once, people barely hear. What you say five times, in different settings, they begin to believe. Say the why in the launch meeting, in one-on-ones, in the hallway, in the weekly update. Each person hears it at a different moment and needs to hear it more than once. Repetition is not nagging; it is how understanding spreads.

**Two-way, not one-way.** Communication is not a broadcast. Build in real channels for questions and worries — a standing time in meetings, an anonymous box, open office hours — and actually respond. When people see their concerns answered, they trust the process more. When their questions are ignored, they fill the silence with fear.

**Share the bad news too.** When the tool makes a mistake, when a deadline slips, when the dip hits, say so. A leader who only shares good news looks like they are selling something. A leader who shares the mess honestly looks trustworthy, and people follow trustworthy leaders through hard patches.

### 21.2 Practical training and side-by-side support

Training is not a one-hour slide deck that everyone forgets by lunch. Effective training is hands-on, practical, and continues long after the first session.

**Train on real work, not toy examples.** People learn a tool by using it on the tasks they actually do. Take a real invoice, a real customer email, a real report (anonymize any personal data first), and have everyone practice on it. A generic demo of "features" teaches nothing. Doing your own job with the new tool teaches everything.

**Teach the judgment, not just the buttons.** Anyone can learn to click "generate." The hard, valuable skill is knowing when the output is good, when it is wrong, and what to do about it. Spend most of the training on review: here is a draft the AI made, what is right, what is wrong, what would you change, and why. This is the skill that keeps bad output from reaching a customer. It is more important than the tool itself.

**Use champions.** Pick two or three respected, curious people to learn the tool first and well. They become the go-to helpers for their peers. People are far more comfortable asking a colleague "how do I do this?" than asking the boss or reading a manual. Champions spread confidence faster than any formal training can. Choose people others already trust, not just the most technical.

**Side-by-side, not hands-off.** In the early weeks, sit with people. Watch them work. Catch the small confusions before they become habits. A five-minute "let me show you" in the moment is worth more than a written guide nobody reads. This is the "side-by-side support" that turns confusion into competence.

**Make help easy to reach.** Create a simple place for questions — a shared document of common answers, a chat channel, a weekly clinic. If getting help is easy, people get help and keep moving. If it is hard, they get stuck, get frustrated, and quietly give up.

### 21.3 Resistance and fear

Resistance is not a character flaw in your people. It is predictable, normal information. It tells you where the plan is weak, where the fear is, and where you have not yet explained enough. Treat it as a signal to listen to, not a problem to crush.

**Name the fear.** Most resistance is fear wearing a costume. "The tool is too complicated" often means "I am afraid I will look stupid learning it." "I don't trust its output" often means "I am afraid of being replaced and this is a respectable objection." When you respond only to the surface argument, the fear underneath stays and keeps working. Address the fear directly and kindly: reassure about the job, reassure that confusion is temporary, reassure that they are valued.

**Resistance is a form of information.** When several people resist the same thing, they are usually pointing at something real. Maybe the tool genuinely does not fit a certain task. Maybe a deadline is unrealistic. Maybe a concern you had not considered is valid. Ask "what specifically is wrong here?" and listen. Sometimes the resisters are right, and catching that early saves you from a costly mistake.

**Do not mock or force.** Dismissing someone as a "Luddite" or a "blocker" poisons the whole team and hardens the resistance. Forcing compliance produces fake compliance — people who use the tool when watched and avoid it when not. Both backfire. The way through resistance is patience, honesty, involvement, and proof.

**Bring the skeptics in.** The strongest move against resistance is to give the skeptics a real role. "You think this will not work — I want you to test it hard and report back honestly." A skeptic who is heard and taken seriously often becomes the most credible advocate, because when *they* say it works, everyone believes it. Someone who is ignored stays an enemy.

**Accept that not everyone comes.** Even with the best handling, a few people may never embrace the change. That is normal and survivable. Focus your energy on the majority who are willing but uncertain. Do not let a small loud minority drain you from the large quiet middle that just needs support.

### 21.4 New processes and new habits

A tool is not a process. A process is the agreed way work flows through your team. When you add AI, you are changing the process, and processes run on habits. Building new habits takes deliberate design and time.

**Redesign the workflow, do not just bolt on the tool.** Do not say "keep doing everything the same, but also use this." Decide the new flow explicitly: what does the AI do, what does the human do, in what order, and where does the handoff happen? Write it down simply. For example: "AI drafts the reply → staff reviews and edits → staff sends → staff logs any error." Clear steps become a habit. Vague instructions never do.

**Make the right thing the easy thing.** Habits follow the path of least effort. If using the AI tool is the default and the easiest route, people will use it. If the old way is easier and the new way adds friction, they will drift back. Remove friction from the new path and, if needed, add a little friction to the old one. Design the environment so the desired behavior is the natural one.

**Expect the dip and hold steady.** As covered in the history, performance drops before it rises. In weeks two and three, people will be slower, clumsier, and grumpier than they were with the old way. This is the dip. If you panic and retreat, you teach the team to give up at the first discomfort. If you name it, expect it, and hold the line with support, you come out the other side faster and better. Tell people the dip is coming before it arrives, so when it does, they recognize it instead of quitting.

**Reinforce, reinforce, reinforce.** A habit sticks when it is rewarded and repeated. Celebrate early wins publicly. Thank people who use the tool well. Show the results — the hours saved, the errors caught. Reinforcement is the "refreeze" step: it turns the new way from a forced effort into the automatic norm. Without reinforcement, habits dissolve back to the old default.

**Make it official.** Once the new way works, write it into your standard procedures, your onboarding for new hires, your checklists. When the new process is "how we do things here," it survives turnover and becomes permanent. If it stays informal, it fades.

### 21.5 Continuous support

The biggest mistake in change management is treating launch as the finish line. It is the starting line. Support after launch is what determines whether the change sticks or slowly erodes.

**Support does not end at training.** People hit new confusions weeks later, when real edge cases arrive. Keep the help channels open. Keep the champions available. Keep the weekly clinic running. The team that gets support only in week one and is abandoned in month two will quietly regress.

**Check in regularly.** Do not assume silence means success. Silence often means people are struggling and giving up quietly. Ask directly, in one-on-ones and team meetings: "How is it going? What is still hard? What is not working?" You cannot fix what you do not know about.

**Keep sharing results.** Show the team what the change is producing — time saved, errors down, customers happier. Progress is motivating. When people can see that their effort is paying off, they keep going. When they see nothing, they wonder if it was worth it.

**Fix what breaks, fast.** When the tool fails or the process snags, respond quickly. Every unresolved problem is a small argument for going back to the old way. Fast fixes say, "this is real, we are serious, it is worth it." Slow fixes say the opposite.

**Keep improving.** The change is never "done." As people use the tool, they find better ways, new uses, and gaps. Keep collecting feedback and refining. A change that keeps getting a little better stays alive; a change that is frozen at launch slowly dies. The measurement that drives this improvement is covered in [Chapter 22 — Measuring Results and ROI](ch22-measuring-results-and-roi.md).

## Ethics and Responsibility

Change management is where ethics and people meet most directly. You are handling people's livelihoods, their pride, and their trust.

**Be honest about the job.** Never promise "nothing will change" if it will. Never hide a plan to reduce staff behind a tool launch. If roles will change, tell people clearly and give them time and support to adapt. A lie about jobs, discovered later, destroys trust permanently and can hurt people who could have prepared.

**Do not use the tool as a cover for cuts you already decided.** If you have already decided to reduce headcount, do not dress it up as "adopting AI." Decide the people question honestly and separately, as covered in [Chapter 16](ch16-goals-costs-and-return-on-investment.md), and be transparent about it.

**Respect the fear, do not exploit it.** People's anxiety about their jobs is real and vulnerable. Treat it with care and dignity. Do not use fear as a motivator ("keep up or be left behind"). Fear-driven adoption produces stress, resentment, and turnover.

**Give people a real voice.** Involving people is not theater. If you ask for their input, be willing to act on it and to say so. Asking and then ignoring is worse than not asking, because it teaches people that their voice does not matter.

**Protect dignity through the dip.** The awkward middle is when people feel least competent. Be patient and kind. Do not publicly shame anyone for being slow to learn. Support them through it. How you treat people when they are struggling is how they will remember this change.

**Honor the endowment.** As the curiosity showed, people are attached to their old skills and routines. Respect that. Frame the change as adding to them, not discarding them. Carry their pride forward into the new way.

## Mistakes to Avoid

**The one-email launch.** Announcing the change once and expecting it to stick. People need the why repeated many times, in many settings.

**Skipping the why.** Telling people what to do without explaining why. Without a reason, change feels arbitrary and breeds resistance.

**Lying about jobs.** Saying "nothing will change" when it will. When reality contradicts the promise, all trust is lost.

**Training on toy examples.** Generic feature demos instead of hands-on practice on real work. Teaches nothing that sticks.

**Ignoring the fear.** Answering the surface objection and leaving the real fear of replacement untouched underneath.

**Mocking resisters.** Calling people Luddites or blockers. Hardens resistance and poisons the team.

**Treating launch as the finish line.** Abandoning support after week one. The change erodes without continuous support.

**Not expecting the dip.** Panicking when people get slower in week two and retreating. The dip is normal; hold steady through it.

**No reinforcement.** Failing to celebrate wins and show results. Habits dissolve without reinforcement.

**Leaving the new way informal.** Never writing it into standard procedure. It fades with the next bit of turnover.

**One-way communication only.** Broadcasting and never listening. Questions go unanswered and fear fills the silence.

**Forcing fake compliance.** Making people use the tool when watched. They avoid it when not. Real adoption cannot be forced.

## Practical Exercise

### 21.6 Exercise: a communication plan

Before you launch any AI change, write a one-page communication plan. Fill in these seven parts.

**1. The why (one paragraph).** In plain words, what problem are you solving and why does it matter to the team and the customers? Write it so a new hire could understand it. If you cannot state the why simply, you are not ready to launch.

**2. The honest job answer (two or three sentences).** What will this change mean for people's jobs? Say it truthfully. If the freed time goes to growth and better work, say so. If some roles will change, say so and say how you will handle it fairly. Write the exact words you will use when someone asks, "Will I lose my job?"

**3. The audiences.** List who needs to hear this: all staff, managers, the specific team affected, customers if relevant. Each audience may need a slightly different message.

**4. The channels and timing.** Where and when will you say it? Plan at least: a launch meeting (the why, the promise, the ask), a follow-up in one-on-ones within a week, a weekly update during the change, and an open question channel that stays open. Write down who says what, to whom, and when.

**5. The champions.** Name two or three respected people who will try the tool first and help their peers. Note why each is trusted.

**6. The dip warning.** Write the sentence you will use when things get slower: "This is the normal dip; it happens to everyone; keep going, support is here." Decide now so you say it calmly later instead of panicking.

**7. The feedback loop.** How will people raise concerns, and how will you respond? Name the channel and how often you will answer.

Read the page back and ask: if I were a nervous employee receiving this, would I feel informed, safe, and included? If not, revise until you would. Keep the plan and follow it. This single page prevents most of the failures listed above.

## Checklist

### 21.7 Change checklist

Before and during any AI change, check these.

- [ ] **You can state the why in one plain paragraph** that anyone could understand.
- [ ] **You have an honest answer to "will I lose my job?"** and you are ready to say it.
- [ ] **You did not promise "nothing will change"** if something will.
- [ ] **You have named the audiences** and tailored the message to each.
- [ ] **You planned a launch meeting** covering the why, the promise, and the ask.
- [ ] **You will repeat the why** many times, in multiple settings.
- [ ] **You have two or three trusted champions** trying the tool first.
- [ ] **Training is hands-on, on real work**, not a generic feature demo.
- [ ] **You taught review and judgment**, not just which buttons to press.
- [ ] **You set a clear rule** for human review before anything goes out.
- [ ] **You have an easy help channel** (chat, doc, clinic) that stays open.
- [ ] **You warned the team about the dip** before it arrives.
- [ ] **You treat resistance as information** and ask what is really wrong.
- [ ] **You bring skeptics in** with a real role instead of pushing them away.
- [ ] **You check in regularly** instead of assuming silence means success.
- [ ] **You share results** (time saved, errors down) to keep motivation up.
- [ ] **You fix problems fast** so the new way stays credible.
- [ ] **You reinforce wins** with celebration and visible progress.
- [ ] **You wrote the new process** into standard procedure and onboarding.
- [ ] **You treat support as continuous**, not something that ends at launch.

If a box is empty, the change is not fully supported. Fill it before you launch, or fill it as you go — but do not leave it empty. A change that is communicated honestly, trained well, and supported continuously is a change that sticks.

## Key Takeaways

- People do not resist change; they resist being changed — tell the truth, involve them, and support them, and most will walk with you.
- Communication is a continuous stream, not one email: lead with the why, answer the job question honestly, and repeat it many times.
- Expect the implementation dip — people get slower before they get faster — and hold steady through it instead of retreating.
- Resistance is information and fear is usually the cause; name the fear, listen to the skeptics, and never mock or force.
- Launch is the starting line, not the finish: continuous support, visible results, and writing the new way into standard procedure are what make change stick.
