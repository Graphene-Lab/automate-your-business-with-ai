# Chapter 13 — Defining a Simple AI Strategy

## In Simple Words

A strategy is a plan that connects what you do today to where you want to be. An AI strategy is the same thing, narrowed to one question: how will AI help this business reach its goals over the next year or two?

Many businesses skip this and jump straight to tools. They buy a chatbot, try a writing assistant, sign up for a platform. A few months later they have scattered tools, no clear results, and no idea what to do next. The problem was never the tools. It was the missing plan.

A simple AI strategy fixes that. It does not need to be a thick document. It needs to answer a handful of questions clearly:

- **Where are we going?** What does the business want to achieve?
- **How does AI serve that?** Which goals can AI actually move?
- **What do we do first, and what later?** Quick wins now, bigger projects after.
- **How much do we spend, and where?**
- **Who decides what?**

If you can answer those in a page or two, you have a strategy. The rest of this chapter shows you how to build each answer.

One honest point: a strategy is not a promise of a fixed future. Tools change fast. A good strategy is a direction and a set of priorities, not a rigid script. It should be short enough to update when reality shifts. A plan nobody can hold in their head is a plan nobody follows.

Think of it like planning a road trip. You decide the destination, pick the route, budget for fuel, decide who drives and who navigates, and plan the stops. You do not need to know every pothole. You need enough of a plan that everyone knows where you are going and who is doing what. That is what this chapter gives you for AI.

## A Bit of History

**1960s–1970s: strategy becomes formal.** Business schools introduced planning frameworks — SWOT analysis, long-range planning. Strategy meant a deliberate, written plan to out-position competitors. The habit of "write the plan first" comes from here.

**1990s: IT strategy becomes its own thing.** As computers spread through companies, "IT strategy" emerged: a plan for how technology supports the business. It was often a separate document, written by the tech department, sometimes disconnected from what the business actually needed.

**2000s: digital strategy.** The web forced companies to think about online presence as strategic, not just a website. Digital strategy tried to reconnect technology plans with business goals.

**2010s–2020s: AI strategy is born.** When AI became a real business capability, "AI strategy" appeared as a label. At first it was mostly hype — vendors selling the idea. Then it matured into a practical discipline: a small plan that ties AI projects to business goals, budgets, and governance.

**The recurring lesson.** The biggest failure pattern across all these eras was the same: technology plans made in isolation from the business. The tech team built what was interesting, not what the business needed. A good AI strategy exists to prevent exactly that — to keep AI tied to real business goals, decided by the right people, with clear priorities.

## Curiosity

### 13.7 The trap of "pilot purgatory"

There is a well-known failure pattern in business AI that a good strategy is designed to avoid. Analysts call it **"pilot purgatory."** A company runs one AI pilot after another. Each pilot shows a little promise. None of them ever becomes a real, scaled, money-saving system. The company spends years and money on experiments that never land, stuck in a loop of trying without committing.

This happens for a strategic reason, not a technical one. Pilots are comfortable. They are small, low-risk, and easy to start. Scaling is hard — it needs budget, integration, change management, and owners. Without a strategy that says "after the pilot, we scale these specific things, funded and owned," companies drift from pilot to pilot forever.

A simple strategy breaks the loop by deciding, in advance, what happens after a pilot succeeds: who funds it, who owns it, and how it scales. It treats a pilot as the first step of a plan, not the whole plan. If you take one thing from this curiosity, take this: **plan the scaling before you run the pilot.** (The exact share of pilots that stall is often quoted in industry reports and varies widely; treat any single number as illustrative. The pattern itself is real and common.)

## A Real Business Example

### A regional logistics firm writes a one-page plan

A mid-sized delivery and logistics company wanted to "use AI" but had no direction. Different managers wanted different things. The warehouse wanted better route planning. Sales wanted lead scoring. Customer service wanted a chatbot. Everyone was right, and the result was chaos.

The owner sat down for half a day and wrote a single page. It had four parts.

**Goal.** Cut delivery cost per parcel by 10% within two years while keeping service quality.

**Where AI helps.** Route and load optimization first (biggest cost lever), then demand forecasting, then customer-service automation later.

**Sequence.** Quarter one: one route-optimization pilot on a single region. Quarter two: evaluate and scale if it works. Quarter three: forecasting pilot. Quarter four: decide on customer service.

**Who decides.** The owner approves budget. The operations manager owns the route project. A named analyst runs the data.

That page ended the chaos. Every conversation now started with, "Does this serve the cost-per-parcel goal?" Requests that did not fit were politely deferred. The route pilot worked, scaled, and paid for the rest. The firm did not have a fancy plan. It had a clear one. That was enough.

## How to Do It

### 13.1 Vision and objectives

Start with the end in mind. A vision is a plain sentence about where you want the business to be in a few years, with AI as part of the picture. An objective is a specific, measurable target that moves you toward that vision.

Write the vision first. Keep it short and honest. Not "become an AI-driven leader in our sector" — that is empty. Something like: "Run the same business with less manual work, faster service, and fewer errors, so our small team can do more." That is a real vision you can feel.

Then write two or three objectives. Each must be specific and measurable. "Reduce the time we spend on admin by 30% in 18 months" is an objective. "Improve efficiency" is not. Use numbers and dates.

A good test: could someone outside your business tell whether you hit the objective? If yes, it is well written. If it could be claimed either way, sharpen it.

Keep the vision and objectives tied to the business, not to AI for its own sake. The goal is never "use AI." The goal is a business outcome that AI happens to help reach.

### 13.2 Alignment with the business strategy

This is the most important rule in the chapter: **the AI strategy must serve the business strategy, not the other way around.**

If your business strategy is to compete on price, AI should target cost reduction. If your strategy is to compete on service quality, AI should target faster, better customer handling. If your strategy is growth, AI should help you scale without adding cost. The AI work must pull in the same direction as the business.

To check alignment, take each AI objective and ask: *which business goal does this serve?* If you cannot name one, drop it or reframe it. An AI project that serves no business goal is a hobby, and hobbies do not get budget.

A practical trick: write your business goals at the top of a page. Below each, list the AI projects that support it. If a business goal has no AI support, ask whether AI could help there. If an AI project sits under no business goal, cut it. The visual makes misalignment obvious.

Alignment also means sequencing follows business priority. Do not spend your first year automating something minor while your biggest business problem goes untouched. Let the business's own priorities set the order of AI work.

### 13.3 Quick wins and long projects

A balanced strategy mixes two kinds of work: quick wins and long projects.

**Quick wins** are small, fast, low-risk projects that show a result within weeks or a couple of months. They come from the high-impact, high-ease box of the matrix in [Chapter 12](ch12-where-ai-can-help-your-business.md). Their job is to prove value, build confidence, and create momentum. They also teach your team how to run an AI project cheaply.

**Long projects** are bigger, harder, higher-impact efforts that take many months. They come from the high-impact, low-ease box. They need more budget, more integration, and more change management. They deliver more, but only after the quick wins have proven the approach and built the skills.

The right mix matters. If you only do quick wins, you never tackle the big value and plateau. If you only do long projects, you risk a lot on unproven ground and may lose support before you see results. Start with quick wins, then layer in long projects as confidence and capability grow.

A simple rule: **lead with quick wins, fund long projects with the credibility and savings they create.** The first quick win pays, in money and trust, for the first long project.

### 13.4 A yearly roadmap in 4 quarters

A roadmap turns your priorities into a timeline. A simple yearly one fits on a page and divides the year into four quarters. Here is a pattern that works.

**Quarter 1 — Prove it.** Run one or two quick-win pilots. Pick the easiest high-value targets. Set clear success measures before you start. Goal: a working result and a learning.

**Quarter 2 — Scale the win.** If a pilot worked, roll it out across the business. Fix what broke. Train the people. Goal: the quick win becomes normal operations and starts saving real money.

**Quarter 3 — Go deeper.** Start the first long project — a high-impact, harder one. Use the skills and savings from quarters one and two to fund it. Goal: begin the bigger transformation.

**Quarter 4 — Consolidate and plan.** Evaluate everything. Measure results against the objectives. Decide what to scale, stop, or start next year. Write next year's roadmap. Goal: a clear picture and a next plan.

This rhythm — prove, scale, deepen, consolidate — keeps you moving without overreaching. Each quarter builds on the last. Adjust the exact content to your business, but keep the shape: small start, then grow.

Write the roadmap as a simple table: quarter, focus, projects, owner, success measure. Keep it visible to the whole team. A roadmap nobody can see is not a roadmap.

### 13.5 Budget: how much to invest and where

You do not need a huge budget to start. You need a clear one.

**Start small and real.** For a small business, a first-year AI budget might be a few thousand to a few tens of thousands — tool subscriptions, a little integration help, and staff time. The exact number depends on your size and goals. What matters is that it is set on purpose, not drifted into.

**Budget the hidden costs.** The tool price is rarely the whole cost. Add: integration (connecting AI to your existing systems), staff time to run and check it, training, and a little contingency for surprises. Many budgets fail because they counted only the subscription.

**Tie budget to expected return.** For each funded project, estimate what it saves or earns. A project that costs a little and saves a lot earns its budget. A project that costs a lot and saves little does not. The simple ROI method is in [Chapter 16](ch16-goals-costs-and-return-on-investment.md). Use it to decide where the money goes.

**Fund the next step from the last.** Let the savings from quick wins fund the long projects. This keeps the budget self-financing and easy to defend.

**Keep a reserve.** Things go wrong. Hold back a small buffer so a surprise does not kill a good project.

A good budget sentence: *"We will invest [amount] this year, mostly in [areas], expecting [result], with [reserve] held back for surprises."* If you can say that, your budget is clear.

### 13.6 Governance: who decides what

Governance is a fancy word for a simple thing: who makes which decisions, and who is accountable. Without it, AI projects drift, stall, or cause harm with no one owning the result.

Define a few clear roles. In a small business, one person can hold several.

- **The sponsor / owner.** Usually you or a senior leader. Approves the strategy and the budget. Settles disputes. Owns the overall result.
- **The project owner.** Runs a specific project day to day. Responsible for it succeeding.
- **The data / technical lead.** Handles the data and the tools. Someone who understands what is possible and what is risky.
- **The reviewer.** Checks that projects follow the rules — privacy, fairness, security. In a small firm this can be the owner or an outside advisor.

Write down, for each kind of decision, who makes it: budget, scope, go/no-go on scaling, handling of a problem. When everyone knows their role, decisions happen fast and no one is surprised.

Two governance habits matter most. First, **regular check-ins** — a short standing meeting to review progress and decide next steps. Second, **clear escalation** — if something goes wrong, everyone knows who to tell and what to do. These two habits prevent most governance failures.

Governance is not bureaucracy. It is just making sure the right person decides the right things and owns the outcome.

## Ethics and Responsibility

A strategy sets the ethical tone for everything that follows.

**Put ethics in the plan, not as an afterthought.** Decide in advance which uses you will and will not pursue, and what checks every project must pass. The principles are in [Chapter 4](ch04-ethical-ai-doing-the-right-thing.md); the law in [Chapter 5](ch05-rules-and-legal-responsibility.md). Bake them into your go/no-go criteria.

**Name accountability for harm.** If an AI decision hurts a customer or an employee, who is responsible? Decide now, not in a crisis. Never let accountability blur into "the system did it."

**Budget for doing it right.** Ethics has a cost — review time, testing, sometimes slower rollout. Put it in the budget so it is not cut when pressure rises.

**Be transparent with your people.** Tell staff what AI will and will not do, and how their roles may change. Hiding the plan breeds fear and resistance. The communication side is in [Chapter 21](ch21-managing-change-in-your-company.md).

## Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Buying tools before writing a plan.** The classic error. Plan first, buy second.
2. **A strategy that is not tied to a business goal.** AI for its own sake is a hobby, not a strategy.
3. **A plan too big to understand.** If no one can hold it in their head, no one follows it. Keep it to a page or two.
4. **Only quick wins, or only big projects.** You need both, sequenced right.
5. **No budget for hidden costs.** Integration, training, and staff time are real. Count them.
6. **No named owners.** Projects without owners stall. Every project needs a person accountable.
7. **Pilot purgatory.** Running endless pilots with no plan to scale. Plan the scaling before the pilot.
8. **Treating the strategy as fixed.** Tools change fast. Update the plan when reality shifts; a stale plan misleads.
9. **Skipping governance.** No decision-maker means no decisions, or bad ones with no owner.

## Practical Exercise

### 13.7 Write your SMART goals

SMART is a simple test for a good objective. Each letter is a quality:

- **S — Specific.** Clear about what exactly you want.
- **M — Measurable.** You can count it or check it.
- **A — Achievable.** Realistic with your resources.
- **R — Relevant.** It serves a real business goal.
- **T — Time-bound.** It has a deadline.

Take one hour. Write **three AI goals** for your business and make each one SMART.

Weak: "Use AI to improve customer service."
SMART: "Cut average first-reply time to customer emails from 6 hours to under 1 hour by the end of Q2, using an AI drafting assistant, with staff review before sending."

Check each of your three goals against all five letters. If it fails one, rewrite it until it passes all five.

Then, under each goal, write: the first project that serves it, who owns it, and roughly what it costs. Now your goals are not wishes — they are a starting plan.

Keep the three goals on one page. That page is the heart of your AI strategy.

## Checklist

### 13.8 Strategic checklist

Before you call your AI strategy done, check these.

- [ ] **You have a one-sentence vision** that is honest and concrete.
- [ ] **You have 2–3 measurable objectives** with numbers and dates.
- [ ] **Every AI objective serves a named business goal** (alignment).
- [ ] **You have quick wins identified** from the impact/ease matrix.
- [ ] **You have at least one long project** planned for after the quick wins.
- [ ] **You have a 4-quarter roadmap** — prove, scale, deepen, consolidate.
- [ ] **You have a budget** that includes hidden costs (integration, training, staff time).
- [ ] **You have a small reserve** for surprises.
- [ ] **Each project has a named owner** accountable for its result.
- [ ] **You have a reviewer** for privacy, fairness, and security.
- [ ] **You have regular check-ins and a clear escalation path.**
- [ ] **You planned how to scale each pilot before running it** (avoid pilot purgatory).
- [ ] **The whole strategy fits on one or two pages** and people can actually follow it.
- [ ] **You will review and update it** as tools and reality change.

If a box is empty, that is your next task. A strategy is done when the right people know the goal, the order, the budget, and their own role.

## Key Takeaways

- An AI strategy is a short plan that ties AI work to real business goals — plan first, buy tools second.
- The golden rule: AI serves the business strategy, never the reverse; if a project serves no business goal, cut it.
- Lead with quick wins to build trust and savings, then fund the harder long projects with what they create.
- A simple 4-quarter roadmap — prove, scale, deepen, consolidate — keeps you moving without overreaching.
- Name the owners and the budget, plan the scaling before the pilot, and keep the whole thing short enough to actually follow.
