# Appendix F — Internal Communication Plan

Most AI projects fail on people, not technology. If staff hear about a change late, or not at all, they resist it. This plan makes sure the right people hear the right message, from the right person, at the right time.

## Why it matters

- People fear what they do not understand. Clear messages reduce fear.
- Staff who feel informed become supporters, not obstacles.
- Good communication prevents shadow AI — people using hidden tools because the official path is unclear.

## Column guide

- **Audience** — Who needs to know. Be specific.
- **Message** — The core point, in one or two sentences.
- **Channel** — Meeting, email, chat, intranet, one-to-one.
- **Owner** — Who delivers it. Sensitive news comes from a person, not a memo.
- **Timing** — When it goes out. Tell people before, not after.
- **Frequency** — Once, weekly, at each milestone.

## Suggested timing for a change

1. **Before** — Announce the change and the reason. Invite questions.
2. **At start** — Explain what changes day-to-day and what support exists.
3. **During** — Share early wins and fix worries quickly.
4. **After** — Report results and next steps.

## Example messages

**To all staff (announcement, from the owner, at a team meeting):**
> "We are starting to use an AI tool to help with [task]. It handles the boring, repetitive part so you can focus on the work that needs your judgement. No one is being replaced by this. We will train everyone and keep a human in charge of every important decision. Questions welcome, any time."

**To managers (email, before rollout):**
> "From [date], your team will use [tool] for [task]. Your job: encourage use, watch for problems, and send feedback to [name] weekly. Please do not let anyone paste customer personal data into unapproved tools. Use only the approved one."

**To the affected team (one-to-one or small group):**
> "This changes how you do [task]. Instead of [old way], you will [new way]. It should save about [X] hours a week. Here is the training on [date]. If the tool gets something wrong, flag it — you are still the one who checks."

**To IT / security (email, early):**
> "We plan to use [tool] for [process]. Please review it against our security and data rules before we start. Check the vendor checklist and the data inventory. Tell us what must be locked down."

**To customers (only if the change touches them):**
> "We now use AI to respond faster to your requests. A real person still reviews important answers. If you prefer to speak to a person, just ask."

## Rules for good messages

- [ ] Say **why** before **what**.
- [ ] Be honest about what changes and what does not.
- [ ] Never hide a change and spring it on people.
- [ ] Give a clear place to ask questions.
- [ ] Repeat the message; one email is not enough.
- [ ] Celebrate early wins publicly to build trust.
- [ ] Listen and act on feedback — silence kills adoption.

## What not to say

- Do not promise the tool is perfect. It is not.
- Do not say "this changes nothing" if it does.
- Do not blame the tool when it errs; own the process.
- Do not announce layoffs disguised as "efficiency." Be direct if roles will change, and how you will help.
- Do not spring a change on people mid-week with no training.
